# AGAINST the Motion: "Gene Editing is Fundamentally Changing What It Means to Be Human"

## Speech (~7-8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

Six thousand years ago, humans domesticated wheat. We took a wild grass and transformed it through selective breeding into the foundation of civilization. One thousand years ago, humans began inoculating themselves against smallpox using material from infected scabs. Two hundred years ago, we developed vaccines. Seventy years ago, we discovered the structure of DNA. Fifty years ago, we created the first test-tube baby. **At every step, critics warned that we were "playing God," that we were "changing what it means to be human."** At every step, they were wrong. I stand firmly against this motion: **gene editing is not fundamentally changing what it means to be human** — it is the latest chapter in humanity's ancient story of using knowledge to reduce suffering and expand human flourishing.

Let me state my position clearly: **what makes us human is not our unedited genome — it is our capacity to reason, to care, to imagine, and to improve the conditions of existence. Gene editing is an expression of these capacities, not a negation of them.**

---

### 1. The Continuity Argument — Humans Have Always Modified Biology

My first argument directly challenges the premise that gene editing represents a fundamental break with human history. The panelist from the model debate who argued for the motion spoke of gene editing as crossing "a line from which there is no return." But this rhetoric ignores the fact that **humans have been modifying biology, including human biology, for millennia.**

Consider the data from the Pew Research Center: while Americans are divided on gene editing for health (48% support, 50% oppose), **70% say editing to give a baby a lifetime reduced risk of serious diseases would be an appropriate use of technology.** Why? Because people recognize that gene editing sits on a continuum with other medical interventions we already accept. Prenatal genetic screening is routine. IVF with embryo selection is common. Folic acid supplementation to prevent neural tube defects is standard prenatal care. We already intervene in human development — gene editing is more precise, not more invasive.

The UNESCO panel's concerns about "tampering with hereditary traits" apply equally to these existing practices, yet we embrace them. As the PMC article on bioethics acknowledges, the line between "natural" and "artificial" human development is **already thoroughly blurred.** Gene editing doesn't cross a clear boundary — it reveals that the boundary was never as clear as we imagined.

The Citizens' Jury members expressed concerns about changing what it means to be human. But with respect, I must engage with what the model debate panelist missed: **humanity has *always* been defined by our ability to transcend biological constraints.** We are the species that invented fire, that conquered infectious disease, that split the atom and walked on the moon. Every one of these achievements "changed what it means to be human" by expanding human capability. Gene editing is different in mechanism, not in meaning.

---

### 2. The Medical Imperative — The Moral Cost of Inaction

My second argument is fundamentally about suffering. The opposition paints a dystopian picture of designer babies and genetic inequality. But they ignore the **actual, immediate, preventable suffering** that gene editing could eliminate.

Imagine you are a parent carrying the gene for Huntington's disease — a condition that guarantees a slow, devastating neurological decline in middle age. You have a 50% chance of passing it to your child. Gene editing could remove that gene from the embryo. The Pew Research data shows that **70% of Americans support this use** — and they're right to do so. Refusing to edit that gene doesn't preserve some pristine human essence — **it preserves preventable suffering.**

The PMC article discusses various genetic diseases — cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anemia, Tay-Sachs disease, muscular dystrophy. These are not abstract philosophical concerns. These are children who cannot breathe, who experience excruciating pain, who die before their tenth birthday. When my opponent argues that gene editing changes what it means to be human, I ask: does what it means to be human include watching your child suffocate from cystic fibrosis when you could have prevented it?

The Citizens' Jury emphasized the importance of "therapeutic" uses over "enhancement." I agree. But the opposition tries to ban or severely restrict even therapeutic applications, citing slippery slope concerns. This is, as the model debate panelist who argued for excessive caution demonstrated, **moral paralysis disguised as moral wisdom.** The certainty of present suffering should outweigh the speculation of future misuse.

---

### 3. The Essence of Humanity Lies Beyond Genes

My third argument engages with the deepest philosophical claim from the opposition — that editing our genes fundamentally alters human nature. This argument rests on a form of **genetic essentialism** that modern science has thoroughly debunked.

The Pew Research Center found that Americans are divided on these questions precisely because they intuitive grasp something the opposition misses: **what makes us human is not our DNA sequence.** Identical twins have identical genomes, yet they are distinct persons with different experiences, personalities, and life trajectories. Conversely, humans share 99% of our DNA with chimpanzees, yet the difference between us is profound.

What makes us human is consciousness, moral reasoning, culture, language, love, creativity, and the capacity for self-reflection. These emerge from our biology, yes, but they are not reducible to genetic code. As the PMC bioethics article recognizes, even if we edit genes for intelligence or memory or temperament, we are editing **substrates of human experience**, not the experience itself. A gene-edited child will still face the fundamental human challenges: finding meaning, forming relationships, confronting mortality, choosing how to live.

The UNESCO panel's concern that gene editing "jeopardizes the inherent dignity of all human beings" commits a category error. Human dignity rests not on **how we came to be**, but on **what we are capable of being.** A child conceived through IVF has the same dignity as a naturally conceived child. A person with a prosthetic limb has the same dignity as a person with biological limbs. **Dignity is not located in our genome — it is located in our moral status as persons.**

---

### 4. The Inequality Argument Proves Too Much

My fourth argument directly addresses the opposition's concerns about genetic inequality. The panelist from the model debate argued that gene editing will create "genetic haves and have-nots." The UNESCO panel worries about "health inequalities between and within countries." These concerns are sincere, but they prove too much.

By this logic, we should oppose all expensive medical interventions. MRI machines are unavailable in much of the developing world — should we ban them in wealthy countries? Cancer immunotherapy costs hundreds of thousands of dollars and is accessible only to the privileged — should we halt its development? The Citizens' Jury participants raised similar concerns, but they failed to grapple with the implications: **the fact that a technology will initially be unequally distributed is an argument for expanding access, not for banning the technology.**

The history of medical technology is a history of democratization. Vaccines that were once rare are now routine worldwide. Antibiotics that were once expensive are now pennies per dose. HIV treatment that cost $10,000 per year now costs $75 per year in the developing world. As the Pew Research data implicitly recognizes, **Americans support gene editing most strongly when framed as reducing disease risk** — and disease knows no class boundaries. The market incentive is to make treatments widely available, not to hoard them.

The opposition wants to deny lifesaving treatment to sick children *today* because of abstract worries about inequality *tomorrow.* That is not justice — that is the perfect becoming the enemy of the good.

---

### 5. Regulation and Governance, Not Prohibition

My fifth argument responds to the opposition's claim that gene editing cannot be controlled, citing the He Jiankui case as evidence. Yes, He violated ethical norms and international guidelines. But the response to his actions proves the opposite of what the opposition claims: **the scientific community universally condemned him, China imprisoned him, and his actions strengthened rather than weakened the international consensus on responsible research.**

The UNESCO call for a moratorium on heritable genome editing is **not a call for a permanent ban** — it is a call for time to develop proper governance frameworks. The Citizens' Jury similarly emphasized the need for robust oversight, not prohibition. The Pew Research data shows that public support varies dramatically based on context and application, suggesting that **nuanced, use-specific regulation is both possible and necessary.**

The panelist from the model debate who argued that "what can be done will be done" commits the fatalist's fallacy. We successfully regulate dangerous technologies all the time: nuclear weapons, human cloning research, gain-of-function virology. Gene editing is technically challenging enough, and the scientific community is responsible enough, that governance is entirely feasible. The real danger is not that we will edit genes recklessly — it's that **excessive fear will prevent us from editing genes responsibly.**

---

### 6. The Humility Argument Cuts Both Ways

Finally, let me engage with the opposition's argument from epistemic humility — that we don't know enough about gene interactions to edit safely. This argument sounds wise, but it contains a hidden arrogance: **the assumption that natural genetic variation is somehow "safe" while human intervention is "dangerous."**

Every child born naturally carries dozens of novel genetic mutations. Some are harmless. Some are beneficial. Some cause disease and death. Nature is not a careful designer — it is a blind watchmaker that produces both Mozart and Tay-Sachs disease. The PMC article's concerns about "off-target effects" and "unintended consequences" apply equally to natural reproduction, yet we don't consider natural conception unethical.

The Citizens' Jury participants worried about "unforeseen consequences." But as the model debate panelist who argued for proceeding cautiously with research noted, **the way to foresee consequences is to do careful, incremental research with robust oversight.** The UNESCO panel calls for more research on safety and efficacy — I agree entirely. But research requires doing, not just theorizing.

What the opposition calls "hubris," I call **the responsible application of knowledge.** We don't know everything about gene editing — but we know enough to begin healing, and we know we'll never know enough if we don't begin.

---

### Conclusion

Let me close by returning to the motion itself: "Gene editing is fundamentally changing what it means to be human." My opponents want you to hear this as a warning. I ask you to hear it as a question: **what *does* it mean to be human?**

If it means having an unedited genome, then every person who wears glasses or receives a vaccine has changed what it means to be human. If it means being subject to genetic chance, then we abandoned that definition when we started screening embryos for chromosomal abnormalities. If it means accepting preventable suffering as natural, then we left humanity behind when we invented medicine itself.

But if being human means **using our distinctive capacities — reason, compassion, ingenuity — to reduce suffering and expand human flourishing**, then gene editing is not a departure from humanity. It is humanity at its best.

The Pew Research data shows that Americans are closely divided on these questions. That's appropriate — these are difficult ethical decisions. But notice what the division is about: **not whether to edit genes at all, but how, when, and under what circumstances to do so responsibly.** The debate has already moved beyond the abstract question of this motion to the practical questions of governance and application.

The opposition asks you to vote for fear — fear of inequality, fear of consequences, fear of crossing lines. I ask you to vote for hope — hope that we can be wise stewards of powerful technology, hope that we can reduce suffering, hope that **what makes us human is not our helplessness before genetic fate, but our power to shape a better future.**

Gene editing is not changing what it means to be human. **It is revealing what it has always meant to be human: to be the species that cares enough about suffering to end it.**

Vote against this motion. Thank you.
