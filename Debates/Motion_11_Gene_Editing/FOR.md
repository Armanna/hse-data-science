# FOR the Motion: "Gene Editing is Fundamentally Changing What It Means to Be Human"

## Speech (~7-8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

For millions of years, humanity has been shaped by chance — by random mutations, by environmental pressures, by the lottery of genetic inheritance. In 2018, that all changed. When Chinese scientist He Jiankui announced the birth of the first gene-edited babies, Lulu and Nana, he didn't just break ethical guidelines — **he broke the evolutionary contract that has defined human existence since our species began.** I stand firmly in support of this motion: **gene editing is fundamentally changing what it means to be human** — not because it threatens to modify our DNA, but because it transforms us from products of nature into products of design.

Let me be clear from the outset: **we are crossing a line from which there is no return, and we are doing so without understanding what we are leaving behind.**

---

### 1. The Redefinition of Human Nature Itself

My first argument addresses the philosophical core of this debate. The **UNESCO International Bioethics Committee** called for a ban on heritable human genome editing precisely because it recognizes what many in the scientific community refuse to acknowledge: that editing the human germline — changes that pass to future generations — represents **"unethical tampering with hereditary traits."** These are not the words of technophobes; these are the conclusions of the world's leading bioethicists after careful deliberation.

When we edit genes that will be inherited by children not yet born, by grandchildren not yet conceived, we are making decisions about the fundamental constitution of future human beings. As one of the model debate panelists correctly observed, this is not like treating a disease in a living patient who can consent. This is **redesigning the human species itself** without the consent of those who will bear the consequences. We are moving from *healing* the sick to *manufacturing* the well.

The Cambridge Citizens' Jury — 13 members of the British public who heard testimony from leading experts — came to a striking conclusion: there should be a **"cautious approach" to heritable genome editing**, with serious concerns about unintended consequences and social inequality. These were ordinary citizens who, after deep engagement with the evidence, recognized what scientists too often ignore: **you cannot fundamentally alter the human genome without fundamentally altering what it means to be human.**

---

### 2. The Illusion of Choice and the Reality of Coercion

My second argument engages with the seductive logic that gene editing merely expands human choice. This argument, often made by proponents of the technology, fundamentally misunderstands the social dynamics that will govern gene editing.

The **Pew Research Center** found that Americans are closely divided on editing a baby's genes to reduce serious health risks — 48% support it, 50% oppose it. But here's the critical insight: once gene editing becomes available, **it stops being a choice and becomes a necessity.** If your neighbor can edit their child's genes to eliminate disease susceptibility, to enhance cognitive function, to improve physical capabilities — can you really choose *not* to? Or will your choice to decline be interpreted as parental neglect?

As the UNESCO panel emphasized, the availability of these technologies in wealthy nations while they remain inaccessible to the developing world will **"further increase health inequalities between and within countries."** The panelist from the model debate who argued against genetic determinism missed this crucial point: gene editing doesn't free us from genetic inequality — it **weaponizes it.** The gap between the genetically enhanced and the genetically "natural" will make every previous form of human inequality look trivial by comparison.

We are creating a world where, as one bioethicist cited in the PMC article warned, there will be **"genetic haves and genetic have-nots"** — a biological caste system written into our DNA.

---

### 3. The Erosion of Human Diversity and Dignity

My third argument concerns what we lose when we gain the power to edit ourselves. The Citizens' Jury participants expressed deep concern about **"changing what it means to be human"** and the potential for gene editing to reduce human diversity. This is not a romantic attachment to the status quo — it is a recognition of a profound truth: **human value has always rested on the principle that we are born, not made; given, not chosen.**

Consider what happens when children become *products* shaped by parental preferences and market forces. The Pew Research data reveals that 72% of Americans believe gene editing for intelligence enhancement would be "taking technology too far." Why? Because when we design our children's traits, we transform them from **persons to be accepted into projects to be optimized.** The unconditional love that has defined parent-child relationships throughout human history becomes conditional on meeting design specifications.

As the UNESCO panel articulated, heritable genome editing **"jeopardizes the inherent and therefore equal dignity of all human beings."** When some humans are designed and others are not, when some carry engineered traits and others carry only the randomness of natural inheritance, we fracture the biological foundation of human equality. **Our genes will no longer be what we share, but what divides us.**

---

### 4. The Argument from Humility — We Don't Know What We Don't Know

My fourth argument is about epistemological humility — about recognizing the vast territory of our ignorance. The panelist from the model debate who supports gene editing often invokes the precision of CRISPR-Cas9 technology. But precision in editing is not the same as precision in predicting outcomes.

The PMC article on bioethics emphasizes the problem of **unintended consequences and off-target effects** in gene editing. We are editing complex systems we barely understand. Genes interact with each other and with environments in ways that scientists are only beginning to map. The gene that increases disease resistance might also affect personality. The edit that enhances memory might compromise emotional regulation. We are operating on the human genome with **a fraction of the knowledge required for responsible action.**

The Citizens' Jury participants repeatedly raised concerns about **"unforeseen consequences"** and the impossibility of fully informed consent when we cannot predict what we are consenting to. As one member put it, we are making irreversible changes to the human species based on **incomplete understanding.** That is not progress — that is hubris.

---

### 5. The Slippery Slope Is Not a Fallacy — It's a Documented Reality

My fifth and final argument addresses those who claim we can limit gene editing to therapeutic purposes. The history of reproductive technology proves otherwise.

When IVF was introduced, critics warned it would lead to designer babies. They were dismissed as alarmists. Today, embryo selection for non-medical traits is routine in many fertility clinics. When prenatal genetic testing became available, it was for detecting serious diseases. Now it's used for sex selection, for screening out traits like deafness even when deaf parents want deaf children. The UNESCO panel's call for an outright ban on heritable genome editing stems from the recognition that **once the technology exists, the slide from therapy to enhancement to transformation is inevitable.**

He Jiankui's experiment with Lulu and Nana was widely condemned — yet it happened. The international moratorium on heritable gene editing has been repeatedly violated. As the Pew Research data indicates, while Americans oppose editing for enhancement, **the line between treatment and enhancement is philosophically incoherent and practically unenforceable.** Is eliminating genetic predisposition to depression treatment or enhancement? What about editing for height, for appearance, for temperament?

The panelist who argued that regulation can contain these technologies ignores the fundamental lesson of technological history: **what can be done will be done.** When China, or Russia, or a rogue fertility clinic decides the benefits outweigh the risks, the global norm collapses. We are not creating a tool we can control — we are releasing a force that will control us.

---

### Conclusion

Let me conclude by engaging directly with the opposition's strongest argument — that gene editing is simply the next step in humanity's long history of self-modification. We have always used technology to extend our capabilities, they say. We use eyeglasses, hearing aids, pacemakers, vaccines. How is gene editing different?

Here is how: **every previous technology has been reversible, individual, and external.** A pacemaker can be removed. Eyeglasses can be discarded. Vaccines don't alter the human genome and don't pass to offspring. Gene editing of the germline is permanent, heritable, and irreversible. It changes not just an individual, but the species itself. It transforms not what we do, but what we are.

The UNESCO panel put it best: heritable genome editing **"would be to intervene in the biological and genetic heritage of future generations."** This is not medicine. This is not enhancement. **This is the replacement of human evolution with human engineering.**

What it means to be human has always included limits — the limit of mortality, the limit of genetic inheritance, the limit of being born rather than designed. Gene editing doesn't expand those limits. **It erases them.** And in erasing them, it erases something essential about the human condition itself.

I urge you to support this motion and to recognize that some lines, once crossed, cannot be uncrossed. Thank you.
