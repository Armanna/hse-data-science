# AGAINST the Motion: "It is environmental factors (parenting styles, educational experiences, cultural backgrounds, etc.) that play a greater role in an individual's development than biological heredity"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

In 1979, two strangers walked into the same office building in Ohio and stopped dead. They were looking at **mirror images of themselves.** Jim Lewis and Jim Springer — identical twins separated at birth and raised by different families with no knowledge of each other — had just been reunited after **39 years apart.**

What researchers discovered was astonishing. Both had been named Jim by their adoptive families. Both had married women named Linda. Both had divorced and remarried women named Betty. Both had sons — one named James Alan, the other James Allan. Both had owned dogs named Toy. Both drove the same model of Chevrolet. Both chain-smoked the same brand of cigarettes. Both had served as part-time sheriffs. Both bit their fingernails and suffered from the same type of migraines.

Different families. Different towns. Different environments. **The same person, twice.**

I stand firmly against this motion. The claim that environmental factors play a **greater role** than biological heredity in individual development is not just scientifically unsupported — it is contradicted by the largest, most rigorous, and most replicated body of evidence in all of behavioral science.

Genes do not explain everything. But when the motion asks which factor plays the **greater** role, the science is unambiguous: **it is biology.**

---

### 1. Behavioral Genetics: The Most Replicated Finding in Psychology

My first argument rests on the single most replicated finding in the history of psychological science — a finding so robust that the field's leading researcher distilled it into **three laws.**

**Eric Turkheimer**, Professor of Psychology at the University of Virginia, formulated what are now known as the **Three Laws of Behavioral Genetics:**

**First Law:** All human behavioral traits are heritable.
**Second Law:** The effect of being raised in the same family is smaller than the effect of genes.
**Third Law:** A substantial portion of the variation in complex human behavioral traits is not accounted for by the effects of genes or families.

Notice what the Second Law tells us: **shared environment — the parenting styles, household income, educational choices, and cultural practices that the FOR side celebrates — accounts for less variation in human traits than genetics does.** This is not one study. This is the consensus conclusion of **thousands of studies**, across dozens of countries, over five decades of research.

The **meta-analysis by Polderman et al. (2015)**, published in **Nature Genetics**, examined virtually all twin studies ever conducted — **2,748 publications**, covering **17,804 traits** across **14.5 million twin pairs.** The result? Across all traits studied, the average heritability was **49%.** Shared environment — the parenting, schooling, and culture the FOR side argues is paramount — accounted for only **17%.**

Let me repeat that. The most comprehensive analysis of human development ever conducted found that genes explain nearly **three times more variation** than the shared family environment the FOR side claims plays the "greater role." This is not ideology. This is the largest dataset in the history of behavioral science.

---

### 2. Twin Studies Reveal the Power of Genes Across Every Domain

My second argument walks you through the specific evidence from twin studies — the gold standard methodology for disentangling nature from nurture.

Identical twins share 100% of their DNA. Fraternal twins share approximately 50%. If environment were the dominant force, identical and fraternal twins raised in the same household would be equally similar — they share the same parents, same schools, same culture, same socioeconomic status. But they are **not** equally similar. Identical twins are consistently, dramatically **more alike** than fraternal twins on virtually every measured trait — and the difference is attributable to the extra 50% of shared DNA.

The **Minnesota Study of Twins Reared Apart (MISTRA)**, led by **Thomas Bouchard** at the University of Minnesota, studied identical twins separated at birth and raised in completely different environments. The results were revelatory:

- **IQ correlation** for identical twins reared apart: **0.75** — nearly as high as identical twins raised together (0.86). Environment contributed remarkably little.
- **Personality traits** (extraversion, neuroticism, openness, conscientiousness, agreeableness): identical twins reared apart showed correlations of **0.40 to 0.60** — meaning that strangers raised in different families, different cities, different cultures were as similar in personality as many siblings raised in the same home.
- **Religiosity, political attitudes, job satisfaction, and even leisure interests** showed significant genetic influence, with heritability estimates ranging from **30% to 60%.**

The FOR side will argue that twin studies also show environmental influence. They are correct — but the motion does not ask whether environment matters. It asks which plays the **greater** role. And across the vast majority of traits — intelligence, personality, mental health, physical health, temperament — **genetics consistently explains more variance than shared environment.**

---

### 3. Adoption Studies Confirm Genetic Primacy

My third argument turns to adoption studies — a natural experiment that the FOR side will wish it could ignore.

Adoption creates a clean separation between genetic and environmental influences. Adopted children share genes with their biological parents but environment with their adoptive parents. If environment plays the greater role, adopted children should resemble their **adoptive** families. If genetics plays the greater role, they should resemble their **biological** families.

The results are remarkably consistent. The **Colorado Adoption Project**, one of the longest-running adoption studies in history, tracked adopted children from infancy through adulthood. By adolescence, adopted children's **IQ scores correlated significantly with their biological parents' IQ** — parents they had never met — but showed **near-zero correlation** with the IQ of the adoptive parents who raised them.

The **Texas Adoption Project**, led by **Joseph Horn**, found the same pattern: adopted children's cognitive abilities correlated **0.28** with their biological mothers and only **0.15** with their adoptive mothers — and this gap **widened** as children grew older. By adulthood, the correlation with biological parents strengthened while the correlation with adoptive parents approached zero.

This finding has been replicated across Scandinavian adoption registries involving **tens of thousands of adoptees.** Danish, Swedish, and Finnish studies consistently show that adopted children's outcomes in education, income, health, and even criminal behavior correlate more strongly with their **biological parents** than with the families that actually raised them.

The FOR side will argue that adoption studies prove environment matters because adopted children do better than they would have in their biological families. And this is true — environment does matter. But the question is **which matters more.** When adopted children raised by affluent, loving families still track the abilities and tendencies of biological parents they have never known, the answer is clear: **genetics plays the greater role.**

---

### 4. Molecular Genetics Is Identifying Specific Genes — and They Matter

My fourth argument brings us to the frontier of modern science — the era of **genome-wide association studies (GWAS)** — which is providing direct molecular confirmation of what twin and adoption studies have long suggested.

In 2018, a groundbreaking GWAS published in **Nature Genetics** by **James Lee et al.** identified over **1,200 genetic variants** associated with educational attainment across a sample of **1.1 million individuals.** A follow-up study in 2022 expanded this to over **3,900 variants** in nearly **3 million people.** Together, these variants form a **polygenic score** that can predict educational attainment with meaningful accuracy — based solely on DNA, with no information about the individual's family, school, or culture.

**Robert Plomin**, one of the world's leading behavioral geneticists and professor at **King's College London**, argues in his book *Blueprint* that DNA is the **"systematic force"** in human development. Environmental events, he contends, are largely random and unsystematic — they matter, but they do not predictably push development in consistent directions. Genetics does.

Polygenic scores now predict not just educational attainment but **cognitive ability, risk for depression, schizophrenia, ADHD, BMI, and personality traits** — all from a saliva sample taken at birth, before any environment has had a chance to act. These are not perfect predictions — they are probabilistic. But they demonstrate something that the FOR side cannot explain away: **DNA alone, with zero environmental information, can predict meaningful variance in life outcomes.**

The FOR side will argue that genes are not destiny — and they are right. But "not destiny" does not mean "less important than environment." A polygenic score calculated from DNA at birth predicts more about a child's educational trajectory than knowing their parents' income, their school's quality, or their country of birth. That is the power of biological heredity.

---

### 5. The Shared Environment Paradox — Why Parenting Matters Less Than We Think

My fifth argument confronts the most uncomfortable finding in all of developmental psychology — a finding that directly undermines the FOR side's central claim about parenting, education, and culture.

**Judith Rich Harris**, in her groundbreaking 1998 book *The Nurture Assumption*, synthesized decades of behavioral genetic research and reached a conclusion that shocked the field: **parenting style has virtually no measurable long-term effect on children's personality or cognitive development**, once genetics are controlled for.

This sounds impossible. But the evidence is overwhelming. When behavioral geneticists decompose the variance in human traits, they consistently find three sources: **genetics** (typically 40-60%), **non-shared environment** (typically 30-50%), and **shared environment** (typically 0-20%). Shared environment is everything that siblings raised in the same household have in common — the same parents, the same parenting style, the same socioeconomic status, the same neighborhood, the same schools. And it explains almost nothing.

What does this mean? It means that two children raised in the same home, by the same parents, with the same educational and cultural experiences, turn out **no more alike than their genetics predict.** The parenting, the schooling, the culture — the entire environmental package the FOR side builds its case upon — explains a small fraction of the variation.

The **non-shared environment** — the unique, random, individual-specific experiences that make siblings different from each other — matters more. But these are not the systematic environmental forces the motion describes. The motion lists "parenting styles, educational experiences, cultural backgrounds" as the environmental factors that supposedly play the greater role. These are all **shared environment** factors. And shared environment is precisely the component that behavioral genetics has shown to be **the weakest predictor of human development.**

**Steven Pinker**, Professor of Psychology at Harvard, summarizes the evidence in *The Blank Slate*: **"The effects of shared environment are small — often negligible — for most psychological traits measured in adults."** The FOR side's entire argument rests on a category of environmental influence that the science shows has the **least impact.**

---

### Conclusion

Let me return to Jim Lewis and Jim Springer — two identical men, separated at birth, raised in different homes, different towns, by different parents with different parenting styles and different educational experiences and different cultural backgrounds.

And yet, when they met at age 39, they were — in temperament, in habits, in preferences, in the very names they gave their children and their dogs — **the same person.**

The FOR side will show you intervention programs that raised IQ by 5 points. I will show you **genetics** that explains a 30-point IQ range. They will show you cultural differences in math scores. I will show you **twin studies** where identical twins separated at birth correlate at 0.75 on IQ — regardless of culture. They will show you epigenetics and claim environment rewrites genes. I will show you **Polderman's meta-analysis of 14.5 million twins** demonstrating that heritability averages 49% while shared environment averages 17%.

The motion claims environmental factors play the **greater** role. Not "an important" role. Not "a significant" role. The **greater** role. And on this question, the science speaks with extraordinary clarity:

**Genetics explains approximately three times more variation in human traits than the shared environmental factors listed in this motion.** Adoption studies confirm it. Twin studies confirm it. Molecular genetics confirms it. And the shared-environment paradox — the finding that parenting style, once genetics are controlled, predicts almost nothing about adult outcomes — demolishes the motion's central premise.

Genes are not destiny. Environment matters. But the motion asks which plays the **greater** role — and the answer, supported by the largest body of evidence in behavioral science, is **biological heredity.**

Vote against this motion. Thank you.
