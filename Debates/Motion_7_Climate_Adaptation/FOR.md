# FOR the Motion: "Adaptation of humans to climate change is occurring fast enough to keep pace with the accelerating, unprecedented scale of climate-related crises"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

In 2023, the Pacific island nation of **Tuvalu** — a country that the world had already written off as doomed to disappear beneath the waves — completed a remarkable engineering project. Using **270,000 cubic meters of sand**, Tuvalu reclaimed **780 meters of coastline**, building new land designed to protect its people against sea-level rise beyond the year 2100. A nation of 11,000 people, with almost no industrial base and a GDP smaller than that of a mid-sized shopping mall, looked at the rising ocean and said: we will **adapt.**

And Tuvalu is not alone. Around the world — from the stilt houses of Bangladesh to the flood-management systems of the Netherlands, from the early warning networks of Malawi to the nature-based coastal restoration projects of Colombia — humanity is adapting to climate change with ingenuity, speed, and determination that the doomsayers consistently underestimate.

I stand firmly in support of this motion: **human adaptation to climate change is occurring fast enough to keep pace with the accelerating scale of climate-related crises.** Not because the crisis is not real — it is — but because the evidence shows that human beings are rising to meet it.

---

### 1. Adaptation Is Happening at Unprecedented Scale

My first argument is about the sheer magnitude of adaptation efforts already underway around the globe.

The **United Nations Development Programme (UNDP)** reports that its climate adaptation projects currently reach **over 164 million people across more than 90 countries.** Since 2002, the UNDP has completed **173 adaptation projects across 79 countries.** These are not pilot programs or academic exercises — they are real-world interventions that are transforming how communities respond to climate threats.

The numbers are striking: **3 million people** are now covered by enhanced early warning systems that did not exist a decade ago. **645,000 people** benefit directly from climate-smart agricultural practices that protect food security in the face of changing rainfall patterns. **473,000 people** have gained improved access to water through climate adaptation infrastructure.

And these are just the UNDP's figures. Add in the work of national governments, the private sector, NGOs, and local communities, and the true scale of global adaptation dwarfs what any single organization can measure.

The panelist from the model debate who argues against the motion will likely focus on what remains to be done. But they must also reckon with what has **already been accomplished** — and the trajectory of acceleration that these numbers represent.

---

### 2. The Economics of Adaptation Are Overwhelmingly Positive

My second argument addresses what may be the most powerful evidence in this debate: **adaptation works economically**, and the world is increasingly recognizing this.

The **World Resources Institute** and the **World Bank** have independently confirmed that every **$1 invested in climate adaptation yields between $2 and $10 in net economic benefits.** The World Bank specifically calculates an average **benefit-cost ratio of 4:1** for adaptation investments. This is not speculation — it is data drawn from hundreds of real-world projects.

**Professor Dr. Patrick Verkooiljen**, CEO of the **Global Center on Adaptation**, puts it plainly: **"Adaptation should not be seen as a cost, but as an investment. Adaptation is good business."** And the market is beginning to agree. The adaptation economy represents a market potential of **$2 trillion annually by 2026** — a figure that is attracting increasing attention from the private sector and driving innovation at commercial scale.

The **IMF's Finance & Development** journal confirms the economics through a different lens: adaptation delivers a **"triple dividend"** — less suffering from future climate shocks, greater economic productivity and growth, and broader social and environmental benefits. When the return on investment is this clear, the question is not whether adaptation will keep pace — it is how quickly the capital will flow to make it happen.

---

### 3. Technology Is Transforming Adaptive Capacity

My third argument highlights the technological revolution that is supercharging humanity's ability to adapt to climate change — a revolution that critics of adaptation consistently fail to account for.

**Professor Auroop R. Ganguly**, Distinguished Professor of Civil and Environmental Engineering at **Northeastern University**, describes the transformation: **"We have or are beginning to have much better tools and methods for predictive understanding and risk analysis."** Artificial intelligence and machine learning are now being deployed to predict precipitation patterns, model flood risks, and optimize disaster response in ways that were science fiction a decade ago.

The **World Resources Institute** has identified six concrete dual-benefit strategies already being deployed globally: decentralized renewable energy systems, sustainable agriculture, climate-smart buildings, mass transit networks, coastal wetland protection, and recognition of Indigenous land management. Their research shows that **over 50% of analyzed adaptation investments also reduce emissions** — meaning adaptation and mitigation are not competing priorities but mutually reinforcing solutions.

Consider the specifics: **white roofing** reflects **60 to 90% of sunlight**, dramatically reducing urban cooling demand. **Silvopasture** — integrating trees with livestock grazing — sequesters **5 to 10 times more carbon** than treeless pastures while protecting farmers from climate impacts. **Coastal wetlands** store **10 to 24 billion metric tons of carbon** while simultaneously providing storm surge protection worth **up to $190 billion per year.**

These are not theoretical proposals. They are technologies and practices being implemented today, at scale, across the world.

---

### 4. Nations Are Innovating Politically and Institutionally

My fourth argument moves beyond technology to the political and institutional innovations that demonstrate humanity's adaptive capacity at the governance level.

In 2023, **Australia** issued the **world's first climate visa** — a new immigration pathway specifically for citizens of Tuvalu threatened by rising sea levels. This is not just a humanitarian gesture; it is a fundamentally new category of political adaptation, recognizing that climate response requires rethinking not just infrastructure but the very frameworks of sovereignty, citizenship, and international obligation.

At the national level, countries like **Bhutan** and **Chad** have finalized comprehensive **National Adaptation Plans** that integrate climate resilience across every sector of government policy. **Cuba** and **Colombia** have pioneered nature-based ecosystem restoration approaches that serve as models for the developing world.

The **Qatar Debate Journal** notes that the justice framework is also evolving: wealthy nations are actively funding adaptation for vulnerable developing countries, recognizing both moral obligation and practical necessity. The adaptation debate referenced by the journal emphasizes that adaptation gives **"direct results to those affected"** — unlike mitigation, whose effects play out over decades, adaptation delivers immediate, tangible improvements in people's lives.

Even the private sector is beginning to mobilize. The **Gold Standard** reports that the adaptation market represents a **$2 trillion annual opportunity**, and while private sector engagement is still in its early stages, the economic incentives are now strong enough to accelerate participation dramatically in the coming years.

---

### 5. Adaptation and Mitigation Are Converging — Not Competing

My fifth argument addresses the false dichotomy that opponents of this motion rely upon: the claim that investing in adaptation means neglecting mitigation — and that therefore adaptation "alone" cannot keep pace.

This framing is fundamentally wrong. As the **World Resources Institute** demonstrates, adaptation and mitigation are increasingly **the same thing.** Their analysis of over 300 adaptation investments found that **nearly 57% of sustainable agriculture investments** showed mitigation co-benefits. Real-world projects confirm this: Uzbekistan's **Resilient Landscapes project** generated **$350 million in emissions reductions** alongside $166.7 million in direct resilience benefits. Colombia's **Heritage Colombia project** delivered over **$1.5 billion in emissions reductions** plus **$1.35 billion in other benefits.**

Professor Ganguly of Northeastern University calls the framing of adaptation versus mitigation a **"false argument"** — both are necessary, and both are happening. The IPCC itself states that **"climate-resilient development integrates adaptation and mitigation to advance sustainable development."**

The motion asks whether adaptation is keeping pace with climate crises. The evidence shows that it is — and that in doing so, it is simultaneously contributing to the mitigation that will slow those crises in the long term. This is not a story of humanity falling behind. It is a story of humanity **innovating its way forward** on multiple fronts simultaneously.

---

### Conclusion

Let me close with the image I began with: a tiny Pacific island nation, building new land from sand to hold back the rising sea.

The critics will tell you that Tuvalu's 780 meters of reclaimed coastline are not enough. That the funding gaps are too large. That the crisis is too vast. And they are right that the challenge is immense. But they are wrong to conclude that humanity is failing to meet it.

**164 million people** are now reached by UNDP adaptation projects across 90 countries. Every dollar invested returns four to ten. Technologies from AI-powered flood prediction to nature-based coastal protection are being deployed at accelerating speed. Nations are innovating — not just technically, but politically, institutionally, and economically.

Human adaptation to climate change has never been about a single silver bullet. It has always been about **thousands of solutions, deployed by millions of people, across every continent and every sector of society.** And that is exactly what is happening — faster, smarter, and at greater scale than at any point in human history.

The question this motion poses is not whether adaptation is perfect. It is whether it is **keeping pace.** The evidence says yes — and the pace is accelerating.

I urge you to support this motion. Thank you.
