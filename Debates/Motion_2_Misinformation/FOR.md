# FOR the Motion: "In modern society, it is hardly possible to stop viral spread of false information in social media"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

In March 2018, researchers at MIT published the largest-ever study on the spread of news online. Their conclusion was chilling: **falsehood travels farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than the truth in every category of information.** False news reaches 1,500 people six times faster than accurate reporting. Lies are 70% more likely to be shared than facts. And here is the most devastating finding of all — this is not because of bots. This is because of **us.** Human beings, by our very nature, prefer the novel, the outrageous, the emotionally charged — and falsehood delivers all three.

I stand in support of this motion: **in modern society, it is hardly possible to stop the viral spread of false information on social media.** Not because we lack the will. Not because we lack the intelligence. But because the very architecture of our digital world — and the very wiring of our brains — makes it structurally, systemically, and perhaps permanently impossible.

---

### 1. The Architecture of Virality Is the Architecture of Falsehood

My first argument is structural. Social media platforms are not designed to spread truth. They are designed to maximize **engagement** — clicks, shares, likes, comments, time on screen. And research from **Northwestern University's Kellogg School of Management** demonstrates that algorithms systematically amplify what they call **PRIME content** — content that is Prestigious, Ingroup-affirming, Moral, and Emotional — regardless of whether it is true or false.

This is not a bug. It is the **business model.** Every major social media platform generates revenue through advertising, and advertising revenue depends on user attention. Sensational, emotionally charged, divisive content captures more attention than calm, verified, nuanced truth. As social psychologist **Jonathan Haidt** puts it: **"Outrage is the key to virality."** The platforms are not neutral pipes — they are amplification engines, and what they amplify most effectively is precisely the content that is most likely to be false.

The panelist from the model debate who argued against the motion suggested that platforms can simply adjust their algorithms. But this ignores a fundamental reality: **an algorithm optimized for truth would be an algorithm optimized against engagement** — and no advertising-funded platform will voluntarily destroy its own revenue model. We are asking the fox to redesign the henhouse.

---

### 2. Human Psychology Works Against Us

My second argument goes deeper than technology — it goes to the very structure of the human mind. The MIT study, led by **Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy, and Sinan Aral**, found that false news inspires **surprise, fear, and disgust** — emotions that are evolutionarily hard-wired to demand attention and provoke action. Truth, by contrast, inspires sadness, anticipation, and trust — quieter emotions that do not compel sharing.

This is not a flaw we can educate away. This is **millions of years of evolution.** Our brains evolved in environments where novel, threatening information could mean the difference between life and death. A rustle in the grass might be a predator — and the humans who **overreacted** to false alarms survived, while those who calmly waited for verification were eaten. Social media has hijacked this ancient survival mechanism. Every notification, every shocking headline, every outrageous claim pulls on the same evolutionary lever. We are fighting misinformation with fact-checks while misinformation is fighting us with **our own neurobiology.**

---

### 3. The Correction Asymmetry — Truth Can Never Catch Up

My third argument is about what happens even when we do identify falsehood: the correction never catches up. Research consistently shows that when explosive misinformation goes viral, its correction is **never as widely viewed or believed.** The outrageous claim is louder, stickier, and more interesting than the follow-up fact-check.

This is known in psychology as the **"continued influence effect"** — once a false belief is planted, even explicit corrections fail to fully erase it. A study published in the journal *Science Direct* found that being fact-checked, having content flagged, or even having content **deleted from one's account** were not effective deterrents to sharing misinformation. People continued to share false information at the same rates. The only thing that actually reduced sharing was the **fear of social isolation** — account freezes or bans. But mass banning users raises its own problems of censorship and free speech.

The panelist from the model debate who supported the motion rightly pointed out this asymmetry. I want to add: it is not merely an asymmetry — it is a **structural impossibility.** You cannot un-ring a bell. Once a falsehood has reached a million minds, no correction on earth can fully undo the damage.

---

### 4. AI and Deepfakes Have Changed the Game Forever

My fourth argument addresses the newest and most terrifying dimension of this problem: **artificial intelligence.** We are no longer fighting human-generated falsehood. We are fighting machine-generated falsehood at industrial scale.

The statistics are staggering. Deepfake files surged from **500,000 in 2023 to 8 million in 2025** — a sixteen-fold increase in just two years. The volume of deepfake videos increases by **900% annually.** A deepfake attempt now occurs **every five minutes.** And here is the critical finding: human detection accuracy for high-quality deepfake video is just **24.5%.** Overall deepfake detection accuracy is **55.54%** — barely better than a coin flip.

This means we have entered an era where falsehood is not only more viral than truth — it is also **indistinguishable** from truth. When anyone with a laptop can produce a photorealistic video of a world leader saying something they never said, and most humans cannot tell it is fake, the very concept of "stopping" misinformation becomes almost absurd. We are bringing a fact-checking knife to an AI-generated gunfight.

As **UNESCO** has warned, we face a fundamental **"crisis of knowing"** — a world in which the distinction between real and fabricated is collapsing. How do you stop the viral spread of falsehood when people can no longer identify what is false?

---

### 5. Fact-Checking Is Losing the War

Finally, let me address the supposed solution: fact-checking. My opponents will argue that fact-checking organizations, community notes, and media literacy programs prove that we can fight back. But the evidence tells a different story.

Research from the **Carnegie Endowment for International Peace** concluded bluntly: **"There is no silver bullet or 'best' policy option, and none of the interventions considered were simultaneously well-studied, very effective, and easy to scale."** Fact-checks are minimally effective at stopping the **spread** of misinformation on social media. And there is a cruel paradox: a study from **Harvard's Kennedy School Misinformation Review** found that exposure to political fact-checks actually **decreases** trust in media and increases perceptions of bias. The very tool meant to fight falsehood is eroding trust in the institutions that produce truth.

Yes, **X's Community Notes** reduced reposts by 46% and likes by 44% on flagged content — and that is real progress. But consider the scale: billions of posts are created every day. Community Notes are applied to a tiny fraction. Even the panelist from the model debate who argued against the motion would have to concede that patching a few leaks while the dam is crumbling is not "stopping" the flood.

---

### Conclusion

Let me leave you with the most haunting line from the MIT study. The researchers found that false news is not spread by bots — it is spread by **people.** By us. We are both the victims and the vectors of misinformation. We share falsehoods because they are novel, because they are emotional, because they confirm what we already believe. And we do this on platforms that are engineered — down to the last pixel and algorithm — to reward exactly this behavior.

Can we slow misinformation? Perhaps. Can we reduce it at the margins? With great effort, yes. But **stop** it? Stop the viral spread of falsehood on platforms designed for virality, consumed by brains wired for novelty, and now powered by AI that can generate lies faster than any human can detect them?

No. Not in this society. Not with these tools. Not with these brains.

I urge you to support this motion. Thank you.
