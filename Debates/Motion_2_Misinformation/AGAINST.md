# AGAINST the Motion: "In modern society, it is hardly possible to stop viral spread of false information in social media"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

In 2014, Finland made a decision that would change the course of its democracy. It embedded **media literacy into the national curriculum for every student, at every age — starting from preschool.** Today, Finland ranks **number one in the world** on the Media Literacy Index — the country with the highest resilience against fake news and misinformation. Not because Finland invented some magical technology. Not because Finland censored the internet. But because Finland decided that stopping misinformation was possible — and then **proved it.**

I stand firmly against this motion. The claim that it is "hardly possible" to stop the viral spread of false information is not just wrong — it is **dangerous.** It is a counsel of despair that serves the interests of those who profit from lies and paralyzes those who could fight back. The truth is: **we have the tools, the knowledge, and the proven models to dramatically reduce the spread of misinformation.** What we sometimes lack is the will.

---

### 1. We Have Already Proven It Can Be Done — At Scale

Let me begin not with theory but with evidence. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the **World Health Organization** partnered with YouTube to implement a misinformation removal policy. The result? **850,000 videos** containing harmful COVID misinformation were removed between February 2020 and January 2021. Not flagged, not labeled — **removed.** This was not a marginal effort. This was a systematic, large-scale intervention that measurably reduced the spread of life-threatening falsehood.

But removal is only one tool. A **Yale University study published in 2025** found that flagging misinformation on social media significantly reduces engagement: posts flagged through X's Community Notes received **46.1% fewer reposts, 44.1% fewer likes, 21.9% fewer replies, and 13.5% fewer views.** These are not trivial numbers. When nearly half of all sharing behavior is eliminated by a single label, that is a powerful intervention.

The panelist from the model debate who argued for the motion claimed that corrections never catch up with lies. But the Community Notes data refutes this directly — when corrections are **attached to the original content** rather than published separately, they dramatically suppress its spread. The problem was never that correction is impossible; it was that we were correcting in the wrong way.

---

### 2. The MIT Study Proves the Problem Is Solvable — If You Read It Correctly

My opponents will undoubtedly cite the famous **MIT study by Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral** showing that falsehood spreads faster than truth. And yes, that is what the study found. But here is what they will not tell you: the same study found that **bots spread true and false news at equal rates.** The asymmetry comes from human behavior — specifically, from the novelty and emotional charge of false content.

Why does this matter? Because human behavior is **not fixed.** It is shaped by education, by norms, by institutional design, and by platform architecture. The fact that the problem is driven by human psychology — not by some unstoppable technological force — means it is **within our power to change.** We change human behavior all the time. We reduced smoking from 42% to 11% of the U.S. adult population. We made seatbelt use nearly universal. We transformed attitudes toward drunk driving in a single generation. If we can change deeply ingrained habits around tobacco and alcohol, we can certainly change how people interact with information online.

A **PMC study on accuracy-nudge interventions** proved this experimentally: simply reminding Twitter users to think about accuracy before sharing **significantly improved the quality of content they subsequently posted.** The intervention was minimal — just a gentle prompt — and yet it worked. Human psychology is not destiny; it is a starting point.

---

### 3. Finland Shows the Way — Education Is the Vaccine

My third argument is that we already have the most powerful weapon against misinformation ever devised: **education.** And we know it works because an entire country has proven it.

Finland integrated media literacy into every level of its education system in 2014. Students learn to identify propaganda techniques, analyze how statistics can mislead, distinguish between disinformation and misinformation, evaluate sources critically, and — crucially — **create their own media** so they understand how content is produced and manipulated. As a result, Finland consistently ranks first in the **European Media Literacy Index,** demonstrating the highest resistance to fake news of any nation studied.

The panelist from the model debate who argued for the motion suggested that education cannot overcome evolutionary psychology. But Finland's results demolish this claim. Finnish citizens are subject to the same evolutionary pressures as everyone else — they have the same brains, the same social media platforms, the same exposure to global misinformation. What they have that others lack is **systematic training in critical thinking from age three.** If misinformation were truly unstoppable, Finland's program would have failed. It did not. It succeeded spectacularly.

This is not an isolated case. Media literacy initiatives are spreading globally — to Estonia, to Sweden, to parts of Asia — and wherever they are implemented seriously, resilience to misinformation increases. The question is not whether education works. It is whether we have the commitment to implement it.

---

### 4. Platform Design Can Be Changed — And It Is Being Changed

My fourth argument challenges the notion that social media platforms are inherently and permanently optimized for falsehood. Yes, engagement-driven algorithms have amplified misinformation. But **algorithms are code, and code can be rewritten.**

The evidence shows this is already happening. **X's Community Notes** — a collaborative fact-checking system where users themselves propose and vet corrections — has been remarkably effective. It increased trust in fact-checking, as a study in **PNAS Nexus** from Oxford Academic confirmed. Unlike top-down content moderation, Community Notes harnesses the collective intelligence of the user base itself.

**TikTok** partnered with AFP in 2020 to deploy professional fact-checkers across multiple countries. **YouTube** launched video fact-checking in the U.S. in 2020, with fact-check panels appearing automatically when users search for sensational topics. **Twitter** labeled **300,000 misleading election posts** in just two weeks around the 2020 U.S. election, limiting their visibility and reducing retweets.

Are these measures perfect? No. Are they sufficient on their own? No. But they demonstrate that platforms **can and do** intervene against misinformation — and that these interventions produce measurable results. The trajectory is toward more tools, not fewer; toward better detection, not worse. My opponents describe a static world in which nothing can be done. The real world is one in which things are already being done — and improving.

---

### 5. Regulation Is Coming — And It Works

My fifth argument is about the regulatory environment. The European Union's **Digital Services Act (DSA),** which came into full effect in 2024, requires large platforms to assess and mitigate systemic risks including the spread of disinformation. It mandates transparency in algorithmic systems, imposes obligations for content moderation, and creates accountability mechanisms with real financial penalties.

This is not theoretical. The EU is the world's largest single market, and platforms that want to operate there must comply. The DSA represents a fundamental shift: from hoping platforms will self-regulate to **requiring** that they act. And the early evidence suggests it is working — platforms are investing more in moderation, providing more data to researchers, and developing more sophisticated detection tools precisely because regulation demands it.

The argument that misinformation is "hardly possible" to stop assumes that the regulatory landscape is frozen. It is not. It is evolving rapidly, and the direction is unmistakable: toward greater accountability, greater transparency, and greater obligation to prevent the spread of falsehood.

---

### 6. Rebutting the Despair Narrative

Let me directly address the defeatism embedded in this motion. My opponents will list everything that has gone wrong — the deepfakes, the algorithmic amplification, the psychological vulnerabilities. And yes, every one of those challenges is real. But notice what they are doing: **they are defining "stopping" as achieving perfection, and then declaring failure because perfection is impossible.**

By that standard, we have also failed to stop murder, theft, fraud, and every other form of human wrongdoing. But no serious person would argue that laws against murder are pointless because murder still exists. We do not demand perfection from our justice system, our healthcare system, or our education system. We demand **meaningful reduction** — and that is exactly what the combination of education, platform design, technology, and regulation is achieving against misinformation.

The question is not whether we can eliminate every single false post from every single platform. The question is whether we can **significantly reduce** the viral spread of misinformation — slow it, contain it, limit its reach and damage. And the answer, backed by evidence from Finland to Yale to the WHO to the European Union, is an emphatic **yes.**

---

### Conclusion

Let me close by returning to where I began — to Finland, to a classroom of five-year-olds learning to ask: "Who made this? Why did they make it? Is this trying to make me feel something? How can I check if this is true?"

Those five-year-olds are the answer to this motion. Not because they are immune to misinformation — no one is. But because they have been given the tools to **resist** it. And they are living proof that the viral spread of false information is not an unstoppable force of nature. It is a **human problem** — created by humans, amplified by human-designed systems, and solvable by human effort, human education, and human ingenuity.

To accept this motion is to surrender. To accept this motion is to tell every fact-checker, every educator, every researcher, every legislator working to protect the truth that their work is futile. I refuse to accept that. The evidence does not support it. History does not support it. And the children of Finland prove it wrong every single day.

Vote against this motion. Thank you.
