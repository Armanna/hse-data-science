# FOR the Motion: "Conventional wisdom about male and female brain has not appeared out of nowhere"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

In February 2024, a team led by **Vinod Menon** at Stanford Medicine published a study that sent shockwaves through the neuroscience community. Using a deep neural network trained on brain imaging data, they achieved something that decades of research had struggled to do: they could distinguish a male brain scan from a female brain scan with **over 90% accuracy.** Not based on size. Not based on stereotypes. Based on patterns of functional brain organization — in the default mode network, the striatum, and the limbic system — that reliably differ between men and women.

This study did not appear out of nowhere. It is the latest link in a chain of evidence stretching back decades — through hormonal research, structural imaging, cognitive testing, and evolutionary biology. And that is precisely what this motion asserts: **conventional wisdom about the male and female brain has not appeared out of nowhere.** It is rooted in real observations, real science, and real biological differences — even if those differences have sometimes been exaggerated or misused.

---

### 1. The Structural Evidence Is Real

Let me begin with brain anatomy. The most replicated finding in neuroscience of sex differences is that **male brains are approximately 11% larger** in total volume than female brains — a finding confirmed across multiple large-scale studies in the UK and beyond. But this is not merely a matter of body size. Research from the **National Institutes of Health** and published in multiple PMC reviews has identified specific regions where sex differences are pronounced: the **amygdala** — involved in emotional processing — tends to be larger in males, while the **hippocampus** — critical for memory — tends to be proportionally larger in females.

Crucially, these regions contain especially **high concentrations of receptors for sex hormones** — estrogen and testosterone — which suggests the differences are not random but are shaped by biological processes during development. Women tend to have a higher proportion of **gray matter**, responsible for processing and cognition, while men have more **white matter**, which facilitates communication between brain regions.

The panelist from the model debate who argued against the motion dismissed these as trivially small differences. But the Stanford 2024 study demonstrates that even if individual differences are subtle, when examined in aggregate and with sophisticated tools, they form **reliable, replicable patterns.** Small does not mean meaningless.

---

### 2. Hormones Shape the Brain Before Birth

My second argument goes to the biological mechanism behind these differences: **hormones.** This is not speculation — it is one of the most well-established findings in developmental neuroscience.

**Simon Baron-Cohen**, professor of developmental psychopathology at Cambridge, has spent decades studying the effects of **fetal testosterone** on brain development. His research, supported by a landmark study published in PNAS involving over half a million participants, proposes the **empathizing–systemizing theory**: that females, on average, score higher on empathizing tasks, while males score higher on systemizing tasks — and that these differences are shaped in part by prenatal hormone exposure.

In utero, male fetuses are exposed to a surge of testosterone that female fetuses are not. This hormonal environment influences the development of brain circuits involved in spatial reasoning, social cognition, and emotional processing. The evidence is not limited to humans: across mammalian species, hormonal manipulation during development produces predictable changes in brain structure and behavior.

The panelist from the model debate who argued against the motion suggested that Baron-Cohen's work has been discredited. This is an overstatement. **Cordelia Fine** in her book *Delusions of Gender* raised legitimate methodological questions — but she herself acknowledged that "we don't yet know whether, on average, males and females are born differently predisposed." That is honest scientific caution, not a refutation. The question remains open, and the hormonal evidence is substantial.

---

### 3. Cognitive Differences Are Consistently Observed

My third argument moves from brain structure to brain function. While the debate about **why** cognitive differences exist is fierce, the **observation** that they exist is remarkably consistent across studies.

Research synthesized in multiple PMC reviews confirms: females perform better on **memory and social cognition tasks**, while males outperform on **spatial processing and motor speed.** The spatial cognition gap — approximately **one standard deviation** — is one of the most robust findings in differential psychology. These differences appear across cultures, across age groups, and across methodologies.

Now, my opponents will correctly point out that these differences are averages, with enormous overlap between the sexes. I agree entirely. But that is exactly what "conventional wisdom" claims — not that every man is better at spatial reasoning than every woman, but that **on average, there are tendencies.** And those tendencies are supported by data. Conventional wisdom, in this case, is not a fairy tale — it is a rough approximation of a real statistical pattern.

---

### 4. Evolutionary Biology Provides a Coherent Framework

My fourth argument draws on evolutionary theory. The conventional wisdom about male and female brains did not emerge in a cultural vacuum — it reflects, however imperfectly, patterns that make sense from an evolutionary perspective.

Throughout human evolutionary history, males and females faced **different selection pressures.** Males, who ranged farther to hunt and compete for mates, would have benefited from enhanced spatial navigation and physical coordination. Females, who bore the primary burden of child-rearing and maintaining social bonds within groups, would have benefited from enhanced social cognition, language processing, and memory.

This is not a "just so" story. It is supported by cross-species comparisons: sex differences in spatial cognition and social behavior appear across primates, rodents, and other mammals, with consistent hormonal underpinnings. The fact that similar patterns appear across species — not just in humans with their complex cultures — suggests that **biology plays a genuine, non-trivial role.**

---

### 5. Acknowledging Misuse Does Not Invalidate the Evidence

Now, let me directly address the strongest counterargument: that the "conventional wisdom" about gendered brains has a dark history. My opponents will point to Victorian phrenology, to the pseudoscientific claim that women's smaller skulls proved intellectual inferiority, to a long tradition of using "brain science" to justify sexism. And they are right — that history is real and shameful.

But here is the logical error in their argument: **the historical misuse of a claim does not make the claim false.** The fact that racists once distorted Darwinian evolution to justify colonialism does not make evolution wrong. The fact that sexists once distorted brain science to deny women education does not mean all sex differences in the brain are fabricated.

**Gina Rippon**, one of the foremost critics of "neurosexism," argues powerfully that we must separate bad science from good science in this field. I agree. But separation means keeping the good science too — and the good science, from Menon's 2024 Stanford AI study to Baron-Cohen's hormonal research to decades of cognitive testing, consistently finds **real, if modest, differences** between male and female brains.

The motion does not claim that every piece of conventional wisdom is correct. It claims that this wisdom has **not appeared out of nowhere** — that it has roots in genuine observation. And the evidence overwhelmingly supports this.

---

### Conclusion

Let me conclude by making a plea for nuance — something that is often lost in this debate. I am not arguing that men and women have fundamentally different brains that determine their destiny. I am not arguing that biology is more important than culture. I am not arguing that any individual should be judged by group averages.

What I am arguing is this: when people observe, across cultures and across centuries, that men and women tend to think, communicate, and process emotions somewhat differently — they are not hallucinating. They are observing patterns that modern neuroscience confirms, however imperfectly. **Vinod Menon's** AI can distinguish male from female brains with 90% accuracy. **Sex hormones** shape brain development from the womb. **Cognitive testing** reveals consistent, if overlapping, differences. **Evolutionary biology** provides a coherent framework for why such differences would exist.

Conventional wisdom about the male and female brain is not always right. It is sometimes exaggerated, sometimes weaponized, sometimes dangerously oversimplified. But it has **not appeared out of nowhere.** It appeared from observation, from experience, and — increasingly — from science.

I urge you to support this motion. Thank you.
