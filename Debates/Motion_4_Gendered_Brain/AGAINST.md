# AGAINST the Motion: "Conventional wisdom about male and female brain has not appeared out of nowhere"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

In the nineteenth century, one of the most prestigious scientists in France, **Paul Broca**, weighed the brains of men and women at autopsy and declared that women's brains were, on average, significantly lighter. His conclusion? That women were intellectually inferior — biologically destined for domesticity, unsuited for education, and incapable of the rational thought that men enjoyed. This was not fringe science. This was the **mainstream scientific establishment,** using the most respected methodology of its time, to reach a conclusion that we now recognize as absurd, bigoted, and utterly wrong.

And yet — here is the critical point — Broca's conclusion felt like common sense to the Victorians. It aligned perfectly with what they already believed. It confirmed their **conventional wisdom.** And that is the dark secret at the heart of this motion: what we call "conventional wisdom" about male and female brains has not come from neutral scientific inquiry. It has come from **prejudice looking for evidence** — and, century after century, finding exactly what it went looking for.

I stand against this motion. **The conventional wisdom about male and female brains has, in the most meaningful sense, appeared out of nowhere — out of thin air, out of bias, out of a long tradition of seeing what we expect to see rather than what is actually there.**

---

### 1. The History Is a History of Pseudoscience

My first argument is historical. The conventional wisdom that men and women have fundamentally different brains is not a product of modern neuroscience. It is a product of **centuries of pseudoscience designed to justify the subordination of women.**

In the Victorian era, **phrenologists** measured skull bumps and declared that women had underdeveloped brain "organs" in areas tied to the arts and sciences, while having larger ones related to child-rearing and religious feeling. This was not science — it was ideology dressed in a lab coat. When brain-weighing replaced phrenology, scientists simply shifted the goalposts: now it was brain **mass** that proved female inferiority. When that too was challenged, the claim shifted to brain **structure,** then to brain **wiring,** then to brain **hormones.**

The content of the claim keeps changing, but the conclusion never does: women's brains are different, and that difference explains why men dominate. As **Gina Rippon** — professor of cognitive neuroimaging — puts it, this is a history of "neurosexism": **the tendency to project cultural gender stereotypes onto neuroscience and then claim those stereotypes are biologically inevitable.**

The panelist from the model debate who argued for the motion presented modern brain research as if it exists in isolation from this history. It does not. Every researcher who studies sex differences in the brain does so in a cultural environment saturated with assumptions about what men and women are "naturally" like. And those assumptions shape what questions are asked, how data is interpreted, and which findings get published.

---

### 2. The "Differences" Are Vanishingly Small

My second argument is about what the actual science says — when you strip away the headlines and look at the data. A **2021 meta-synthesis** of brain imaging studies found that sex accounted for **less than 1% of the variance** in brain structure or laterality. Less than one percent. The only large group-level difference found reliably was in **total brain volume** — men's brains are about 11% larger — which is fully explained by men's larger average body size and has **no demonstrated relationship to intelligence or cognitive ability.**

As for the regional differences that my opponents will cite — the amygdala, the hippocampus, the corpus callosum — a review published in PMC states clearly: **"once brain size is accounted for, sex/gender differences are small and not reliably found across recent large studies."** The differences that are found are typically so small that they are dwarfed by the variation **within** each sex. The differences among women are far greater than the average difference between women and men. The same is true for men.

The panelist from the model debate who argued for the motion cited the Stanford 2024 AI study by Vinod Menon, which achieved 90% accuracy in distinguishing male from female brain scans. But here is what they did not mention: **AI models are designed to find patterns** — even in noise. A model trained to distinguish left-handed from right-handed people, or tall from short people, would also find patterns. The existence of a statistically detectable pattern does not mean that pattern is large, meaningful, or relevant to how people actually think and behave.

---

### 3. The Brain Is a Mosaic, Not a Binary

My third argument draws on one of the most important neuroscience studies of the past decade. In 2015, **Daphna Joel** and her colleagues at Tel Aviv University published a groundbreaking analysis in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. They examined MRI scans from over **1,400 individuals** and found **"extensive overlap between the distributions of females and males"** across all gray matter, white matter, and neural connections assessed.

Their critical finding: **internal consistency was extremely rare.** Very few individuals had brains that were consistently at the "female end" or the "male end" of any spectrum. Instead, each brain was a unique **mosaic** — a mixture of features, some more common in women, some more common in men, arranged in combinations that defied any neat categorization.

Joel's conclusion is devastating for conventional wisdom: **"There is no such thing as a 'male brain' or a 'female brain.'"** There are only human brains, each one unique, each one shaped by a combination of genetics, hormones, experience, and environment that cannot be reduced to a pink-or-blue label.

Yes, **Marco Del Giudice** re-analyzed Joel's data and found that statistical models could classify individuals by sex 69–77% of the time. But even this rebuttal proves Joel's point: **23 to 31% of people cannot be correctly classified** — a massive failure rate for something that conventional wisdom treats as self-evident. If male and female brains were truly as different as conventional wisdom suggests, the accuracy should be 100%, not 70%.

---

### 4. Neuroplasticity Means Culture Shapes the Brain

My fourth argument goes to the heart of the nature-nurture question. Even where sex differences in brain structure or function are observed, **we cannot assume they are innate** — because the brain is not a fixed organ. It is profoundly shaped by experience.

This is the principle of **neuroplasticity**, and its implications for the gendered brain debate are enormous. Research has shown that sex differences in cognitive performance — including the famous spatial cognition gap — can be **dramatically reduced or even eliminated** by training. One study found that differences between men and women on spatial tasks were **largely eliminated after both groups played a video game for only a few hours.**

If a few hours of practice can close a cognitive gap that supposedly reflects deep biological wiring, then the "wiring" was never as deep as claimed. What we are observing is not a biologically fixed male or female brain — it is a brain shaped by a lifetime of gendered experience. Boys are given construction toys; girls are given dolls. Boys are encouraged to explore; girls are encouraged to nurture. These experiences physically rewire the brain through neuroplasticity — and then, when scientists scan the brains of adults and find differences, they call those differences "biological."

**Cordelia Fine** calls this the great circular argument of gendered neuroscience: **society creates gendered experiences → gendered experiences shape the brain → brain differences are cited as evidence that gender is biological → biological gender is used to justify gendered society.** The conventional wisdom is not based on evidence. It is based on a feedback loop.

---

### 5. Baron-Cohen's Theory Is Methodologically Flawed

My fifth argument addresses the most prominent scientific framework supporting gendered brain conventional wisdom: **Simon Baron-Cohen's empathizing–systemizing theory.** Cordelia Fine, in *Delusions of Gender*, subjected this framework to rigorous critique and found serious problems.

First, there are fundamental questions about whether Baron-Cohen is actually measuring **fetal testosterone** — his central explanatory variable — or something else entirely. Second, there are questions about whether his tests actually measure "empathizing" and "systemizing" as coherent psychological constructs, or whether they measure familiarity with socially gendered activities. Third, and most importantly, Fine argues that **it is not even clear that males and females reliably differ in these skills** once you control for social expectations and stereotype threat.

Fine's own words are telling: "The thesis of my book is that while social effects on sex differences are well-established, **spurious results, poor methodologies, and untested assumptions** mean we don't yet know whether, on average, males and females are born differently predisposed to systemizing versus empathizing." This is not a ringing endorsement of the conventional wisdom. It is a warning that the science behind it is far shakier than it appears.

---

### Conclusion

Let me return to where I began — to Paul Broca, weighing brains in nineteenth-century Paris, confirming what he already believed. Every generation has its version of Broca. Every generation finds new scientific language to dress up old prejudices.

The conventional wisdom about male and female brains tells us a satisfying story: that the differences we observe between men and women are natural, inevitable, and hardwired. But when you examine the evidence — really examine it, not the headlines, not the pop-science books, but the data — you find something very different. You find differences that account for **less than 1% of brain variance.** You find brains that are **mosaics, not binaries.** You find cognitive gaps that **disappear with a few hours of training.** You find a leading theory whose methodology has been called **"spurious" and "untested"** by one of the world's foremost experts.

The conventional wisdom about male and female brains has appeared, in the deepest sense, **out of nowhere** — out of cultural expectations, out of confirmation bias, out of a centuries-long tradition of using science to justify inequality. The actual science tells us something far more interesting and far more liberating: that every human brain is unique, that our potential is not determined by our sex, and that the categories we impose on brains say more about **our society** than about our biology.

Vote against this motion. Thank you.
