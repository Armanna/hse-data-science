# AGAINST the Motion: "Social media is a big force working in the society towards damaging democracy"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

In January 2011, a 26-year-old Tunisian named Mohamed Bouazizi set himself on fire to protest government corruption and humiliation. Within weeks, images and videos of the protests that followed spread across Facebook and Twitter, igniting a revolutionary wave across the Arab world. In Egypt, tweet volumes about political change surged from **2,300 per day to 230,000 per day** in the week before Hosni Mubarak's resignation. In Tunisia, political discussion on blogs and Twitter **immediately preceded** mass protests that toppled a dictator who had ruled for 23 years.

The Arab Spring was not a social media revolution — but it was a revolution that social media **made possible.** Without Facebook, without Twitter, without YouTube, millions of citizens living under authoritarian rule would have had no way to coordinate, no way to share their stories with the world, and no way to know that they were not alone. That is not a force damaging democracy. That is a force **creating** it where it did not exist.

I stand firmly against this motion. Social media is not a force working towards damaging democracy — it is **the most powerful tool for democratic participation, accountability, and civic engagement that ordinary citizens have ever possessed.**

---

### 1. Social Media Has Democratized Political Participation

My first argument is about who gets to participate in democracy. For most of democratic history, meaningful political participation was reserved for elites — for those who could afford newspaper advertisements, television airtime, or the connections to reach decision-makers. Social media **shattered** that monopoly.

Today, a teenager in a small town can reach millions of people with a single post about an injustice. A grassroots movement can organize a nationwide protest in hours. A whistleblower can expose government corruption without needing a newspaper editor to approve the story. From **#BlackLivesMatter** to **#MeToo** to **#FridaysForFuture**, social media has enabled movements that would have been impossible in the pre-digital age — movements that have changed laws, toppled powerful abusers, and forced governments to confront systemic injustice.

The data supports this. Research from the **University of Maryland** found that social media positively impacted civic engagement, registration, and early voting in the 2020 U.S. election, with many states reporting **record-breaking new voter registration** numbers and nationwide early voting reaching **99.7 million** — a historic record. A field experiment found that Facebook get-out-the-vote messages increased student turnout by **8.22 percentage points,** surpassing even the impact of face-to-face voter contact.

The panelist from the model debate who argued for the motion dismissed social media activism as "slacktivism." But 99.7 million early voters are not slacktivists. They are citizens exercising their democratic rights — and social media helped them do it.

---

### 2. Social Media Holds Power Accountable

My second argument is about accountability — the lifeblood of democracy. Democratic governance depends on citizens being able to see what their leaders do and hold them responsible. Social media has transformed this capacity.

Before social media, government officials could make decisions behind closed doors, confident that most citizens would never know. Today, every press conference is live-streamed, every vote is tracked, every contradiction between a politician's words and actions can be documented and shared in real time. Social media has created a world of **radical transparency** — where citizens can respond instantaneously, providing feedback on policies and decisions, leading to new forms of public discourse that make governance more interactive and participatory.

Consider the power of **citizen journalism.** When George Floyd was murdered by a police officer in Minneapolis in 2020, it was a bystander's smartphone video — shared on social media — that held power accountable. Without that video, shared millions of times on Facebook, Twitter, and Instagram, there would have been no global reckoning with police violence. The officer would likely have walked free, as so many had before. Social media did not damage democracy that day — it **forced democracy to live up to its promises.**

Platforms like **WikiLeaks** and **SecureDrop** have enabled digital whistleblowing on an unprecedented scale, redistributing power between states, citizens, and corporations. Citizens can now fact-check government claims in real time, amplifying their voices when they feel their rights are being compromised. This is not damage to democracy — this is democracy **working.**

---

### 3. The Polarization Narrative Is Overstated

My third argument challenges the core claim of my opponents: that social media is driving democratic societies apart. The evidence is far more nuanced than the doom narrative suggests.

A landmark **2024 PMC study** examined the effects of deactivating Facebook and Instagram on polarization during the 2020 U.S. election. The results? The effects on affective polarization, issue polarization, perceived legitimacy of the election, candidate favorability, and voter turnout were all **"precisely estimated and close to zero."** Let me repeat that: a controlled, scientific experiment found that social media deactivation had **essentially no effect** on the metrics that matter most for democracy.

Furthermore, a literature review from the **Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism** at Oxford found that algorithmic selection by search engines and social media generally leads to **"slightly more diverse news use — the opposite of the filter bubble hypothesis."** The notion that everyone is trapped in an echo chamber is popular, but the empirical evidence for it is surprisingly weak. Most people encounter a variety of viewpoints online — often more diverse than what they would encounter in their offline social networks.

The panelist from the model debate who argued for the motion relied heavily on the echo chamber narrative. But as the research shows, political polarization in many countries — including the United States — has been **increasing since the 1970s,** long before social media existed. Television, talk radio, and partisan newspapers have been fragmenting the information landscape for decades. Blaming social media for a trend that predates it by 40 years is not just inaccurate — it is intellectually lazy.

---

### 4. Cambridge Analytica Proves the System Can Self-Correct

My opponents will inevitably raise the Cambridge Analytica scandal — and they should. It was a genuine abuse of democratic processes. But I would argue that the Cambridge Analytica story actually **supports** my position, not theirs.

Here is what actually happened: Cambridge Analytica harvested data from 87 million Facebook users without consent. This was exposed — **by journalists using social media** to amplify the story. The resulting public outcry — organized largely **on social media** — led to congressional hearings, a $5 billion FTC fine against Facebook (the largest privacy penalty in history), the dissolution of Cambridge Analytica, the passage of the EU's **GDPR** and the **Digital Services Act**, and a fundamental shift in how the public understands data privacy.

In other words, the democratic system **worked.** The abuse was discovered, publicized, punished, and resulted in new protections. And social media was central to every stage of that democratic response. The claim that Cambridge Analytica proves social media damages democracy is like saying that the Watergate scandal proves journalism damages democracy. Exposure of abuse is not a symptom of democratic failure — it is a sign of democratic health.

---

### 5. The Alternative to Social Media Is Worse for Democracy

My fifth argument asks a question my opponents must answer: **compared to what?** If we removed social media from democratic life, would democracy be stronger? The evidence suggests the opposite.

Before social media, political information was controlled by a small number of media gatekeepers — newspaper editors, television network executives, government press offices. These gatekeepers decided what was newsworthy, which voices were heard, and which stories were buried. This system was not democratic — it was **oligarchic.** It systematically excluded women, minorities, the poor, the young, and anyone who lacked institutional power.

Social media broke that oligarchy. It gave every citizen a platform — imperfect, noisy, sometimes chaotic, but **radically more inclusive** than anything that came before. The **Pew Research Center** has found that social media is now the primary news source for young adults, and research confirms that youth who rely on digital platforms for political information are more likely to engage in civic activities — from volunteering to contacting elected officials.

The panelist from the model debate who argued for the motion painted a nostalgic picture of pre-social-media democracy. But that democracy — where information was controlled by a handful of corporations, where marginalized voices were systematically silenced, where government abuse could be hidden for decades — was not a golden age. It was a **gated community.** Social media opened the gates.

---

### 6. Engaging With the Extremism Argument

Finally, let me directly address the argument about radicalization and extremism. Yes, social media has been used to spread extremist content. Yes, algorithms can amplify harmful material. These are real problems that require real solutions — better moderation, smarter regulation, improved media literacy.

But consider this: extremism existed long before social media. The Ku Klux Klan recruited millions of members through word of mouth and pamphlets. Nazi propaganda spread through radio and cinema. The Rwandan genocide was fueled by a radio station. Every communication technology in history has been exploited by extremists — the printing press, the telephone, television, email. We did not ban any of these technologies. We regulated them, improved them, and kept the enormous democratic benefits they provided.

Social media deserves the same treatment: not condemnation, but **reform.** And reform is already happening — through the EU's Digital Services Act, through platform policies, through community-driven tools like X's Community Notes. The trajectory is toward a better, more accountable social media ecosystem — not a perfect one, but a **better** one.

---

### Conclusion

Let me close by returning to the Arab Spring — and to a broader truth about democracy.

Democracy is messy. It is loud, contentious, and uncomfortable. It involves people you disagree with having the same right to speak as you do. It involves misinformation alongside truth, outrage alongside reason, conflict alongside cooperation. That has been true since the Athenian agora, and it will be true long after the last social media platform shuts down.

Social media has amplified all of democracy's qualities — **both its strengths and its weaknesses.** It has amplified misinformation, yes — but it has also amplified truth, accountability, and the voices of the voiceless. It has amplified outrage, yes — but it has also amplified movements for justice that have changed the world.

The question is not whether social media is perfect. It is not. The question is whether it is, on balance, **a force for democratic empowerment or democratic destruction.** And when I look at 99.7 million early voters, at citizens filming police brutality, at protesters in Tunisia and Egypt finding each other for the first time, at whistleblowers exposing corruption, at Cambridge Analytica being brought to justice — I see a tool that, for all its flaws, has given ordinary people more democratic power than they have ever had in human history.

That is not a force damaging democracy. That is democracy **coming alive.**

Vote against this motion. Thank you.
