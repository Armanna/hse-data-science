# FOR the Motion: "Social media is a big force working in the society towards damaging democracy"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

On January 6th, 2021, a mob stormed the United States Capitol — the very building where the American Constitution has been guarded for over two centuries. They came with zip ties, flagpoles turned into weapons, and the genuine belief that their election had been stolen. Where did that belief come from? Not from evidence — every court, every recount, every audit confirmed the election's legitimacy. It came from **social media** — from months of algorithmically amplified conspiracy theories, echo chambers of rage, and viral misinformation that no fact-checker could contain.

That was not a glitch in the system. That was the system **working as designed.** I stand firmly in support of this motion: **social media is a big force working in society towards damaging democracy.** It fragments public discourse, amplifies extremism, enables manipulation, and erodes the shared foundation of trust on which all democratic governance depends.

---

### 1. The Fragmentation of Shared Reality

My first argument is about something democracy cannot survive without: **a shared sense of reality.** Democracy requires that citizens, despite their disagreements, inhabit the same factual universe — that they can agree on basic truths even when they disagree on policy. Social media is systematically destroying this.

**Jonathan Haidt**, social psychologist at NYU and author of *The Righteous Mind*, describes what is happening with the metaphor of the **Tower of Babel**: "a story about the fragmentation of everything — the shattering of all that had seemed solid, the scattering of people who had been a community." He argues that architectural changes made to Facebook, Twitter, and other platforms between 2009 and 2012 — specifically the introduction of the "like," "share," and "retweet" buttons — fundamentally transformed these platforms from spaces of connection into engines of fragmentation.

Before social media, citizens consumed broadly similar news — from a handful of newspapers and television networks. They argued about the same facts. Today, two voters in the same city can inhabit **entirely different informational universes** — each reinforced by algorithms that serve them content confirming what they already believe, and hiding everything that might challenge them. A **systematic review** of a decade of echo chamber research (2015–2025) found that algorithmic systems **structurally amplify ideological homogeneity,** reinforcing selective exposure and limiting viewpoint diversity.

Without shared facts, democratic deliberation becomes impossible. You cannot compromise with someone who lives in a different reality.

---

### 2. Algorithms Are Engines of Radicalization

My second argument goes to **how** social media fragments reality: through algorithms designed to maximize engagement, which systematically reward extremism over moderation.

The evidence is damning. Research found that **Facebook's recommendation tools were responsible for 64% of all extremist group joins** on the platform. A study on TikTok demonstrated how quickly users can be radicalized: engaging with transphobic content led the algorithm to rapidly increase the volume and variety of **far-right video recommendations.** A 2024 field experiment on X during the U.S. presidential campaign, published in *Science*, provided **causal evidence** that exposure to content expressing antidemocratic attitudes and partisan animosity shifts out-party hostility by more than 2 points on a 100-point scale.

These are not accidents. Social media algorithms are optimized for **engagement** — and the content that generates the most engagement is content that provokes outrage, fear, and moral disgust. As Haidt puts it: political elites have become both more polarized themselves and **more incentivized to stoke polarization** among voters, because extremism is rewarded with likes, shares, and algorithmic amplification. Moderate voices are drowned out not because they are wrong, but because they are **boring.**

The panelist from the model debate who argued against the motion suggested that polarization predates social media. This is true — but it is like saying that fire predated gasoline. Social media did not invent extremism. It **industrialized** it.

---

### 3. Cambridge Analytica: The Blueprint for Democratic Manipulation

My third argument is about the deliberate weaponization of social media against democratic processes. The **Cambridge Analytica scandal** is the defining case study.

In 2018, it was revealed that Cambridge Analytica harvested the personal data of up to **87 million Facebook users** — without their knowledge or consent — through a seemingly innocent personality quiz app. This data was used to build **psychographic profiles** of American voters, determining personality traits based on Facebook activity, and then deploying **micro-targeted political messaging** designed to manipulate individual voters during the 2016 U.S. presidential election and the Brexit referendum.

But Cambridge Analytica was not just a domestic scandal. The firm operated in **over 200 countries,** exporting the same techniques to elections worldwide. And investigations revealed connections between Cambridge Analytica and Russian intelligence: U.S. sensitive polling and election data were passed to Russian intelligence via a Cambridge Analytica contractor and Trump campaign associates.

As **Amnesty International** put it, Cambridge Analytica is "just the tip of the iceberg." The architecture of social media — the vast collection of personal data, the capacity for micro-targeting, the opacity of algorithmic delivery — makes every platform a potential tool for democratic manipulation. The panelist from the model debate who argued for the motion was right to call this an infrastructure problem, not merely a content problem.

---

### 4. The Erosion of Institutional Trust

My fourth argument is perhaps the most corrosive effect of all: social media is systematically eroding **trust in democratic institutions** — the courts, elections, the press, the government, science itself.

Haidt argues that democracy depends on **"widely internalized acceptance of the legitimacy of rules, norms, and institutions."** When citizens lose trust in elected leaders, health authorities, the courts, the police, and the integrity of elections, then "every decision becomes contested; every election becomes a **life-and-death struggle** to save the country from the other side."

Social media accelerates this erosion in two ways. First, it gives every conspiracy theory — no matter how baseless — the same visual credibility and reach as legitimate journalism. A Facebook post claiming the election was stolen looks exactly the same as one from the Associated Press. Second, social media amplifies what researchers call **"perceived polarization"** — making extremist voices more visible while hiding moderate majorities. Current evidence from a **Reuters Institute literature review** suggests that digital media fuels perceived polarization by making extremist voices more salient, causing citizens to believe their society is more divided and their institutions more corrupt than they actually are.

The result is a vicious cycle: distrust drives people to social media for "alternative" information, which exposes them to more misinformation, which deepens their distrust further. Democracy is bleeding from a wound that social media keeps reopening.

---

### 5. Real-World Democratic Damage — A Global Pattern

My fifth argument surveys the global evidence. This is not an American problem. Social media's damage to democracy is a **worldwide phenomenon.**

In **Brazil**, social media was a critical undercurrent in generating social unrest during the 2018 elections and ultimately inciting political violence, leading to the January 8th, 2023 storming of government buildings in Brasilia — a mirror image of January 6th. In **Myanmar**, Facebook was identified by United Nations investigators as having played a "determining role" in the genocide against the Rohingya people, with military propaganda spreading virally through the platform. In **the Philippines**, social media-driven disinformation helped elect authoritarian-leaning leaders. In **India**, WhatsApp rumor mills have incited mob lynchings.

The pattern is consistent across continents, languages, and political systems: social media **amplifies extremism, enables manipulation, and weakens the democratic immune system** — the shared norms, trust, and institutions that allow societies to govern themselves peacefully.

The panelist from the model debate who argued against the motion pointed to the Arab Spring as evidence that social media can empower democratic movements. But even this example ultimately proves my point: in the decade since the Arab Spring, nearly every country that experienced a social media-fueled revolution has descended into authoritarianism, civil war, or chaos. Social media is excellent at **tearing things down.** It is terrible at **building things up.**

---

### Conclusion

Let me close with Jonathan Haidt's most sobering observation. He writes that illiberal movements on the right and the left are "gaining strength and **ramping each other up,** hyper-activating the tribal psychology" that makes democratic governance impossible. Social media is the accelerant.

Democracy is a fragile experiment. It depends on citizens who can disagree without dehumanizing each other, who share enough common ground to compromise, who trust that elections are legitimate even when their side loses. Social media is undermining **every single one** of these preconditions.

It is fragmenting our shared reality. It is rewarding extremism and punishing moderation. It is enabling the manipulation of elections on a global scale. It is eroding trust in every institution democracy needs to function. And it is doing all of this not by accident, but **by design** — because outrage is profitable, and democracy is collateral damage.

I urge you to support this motion. Thank you.
