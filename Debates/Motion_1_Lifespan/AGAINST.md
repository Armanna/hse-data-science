# AGAINST the Motion: "People Are Not Ready to Have a Longer Lifespan"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

Two hundred years ago, the average human being could expect to live to the age of 35. If someone had stood before an audience in 1826 and said, "People are not ready to live to 70," they would have had compelling evidence — rampant disease, no antibiotics, child mortality at 43%. And they would have been **spectacularly wrong.** Humanity was not merely ready — humanity *made itself* ready. We built sanitation systems, invented vaccines, revolutionized agriculture, and created social safety nets. Every generation was told it was not ready for the next leap in longevity. Every generation proved the doubters wrong.

I stand firmly against this motion. **People are not only ready for longer lifespans — they are already living them, and the evidence shows that this is overwhelmingly a story of human triumph, not human failure.**

---

### 1. The Data Is Unambiguous: We Are Already Succeeding

Let me begin with the facts, because facts matter more than fear. The **Stanford study by Shripad Tuljapurkar**, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences and cited in the Futures Platform reading, analyzed 50 years of mortality data across 20 developed countries. What did he find? That the average age of death for those who survive past 65 increases by **three years every generation** — steadily, consistently, across every country studied. Tuljapurkar himself expected to find a slowdown, a ceiling. Instead, he said — and I quote — **"The data shows that we can expect longer lives and there's no sign of a slowdown in this trend."**

This is not a crisis unfolding. This is a success story being written in real time. We are living longer, and the trend is remarkably stable. If we were truly "not ready," we would see the trend collapsing — life expectancies falling, systems buckling under the weight. Instead, **the trend holds across 20 nations over half a century.** That is not the signature of unreadiness. That is the signature of adaptation.

---

### 2. Longer Lives Come With Greater Capability, Not Greater Burden

My opponents will inevitably paint a picture of frail, dependent, expensive old people draining society's resources. This is not only pessimistic — it is **empirically wrong.**

The Futures Platform article cites research from the **Milken Institute's Center for the Future of Aging and the Stanford Center on Longevity** showing that older workers take **fewer sick days**, demonstrate **stronger problem-solving skills**, and are **more likely to report high job satisfaction** than their younger colleagues. Older workers are not a burden — they are an asset. The panelist from the model debate who argued for the motion tried to present aging as decline, but the evidence shows the opposite: aging, in an era of advancing medicine, increasingly means **accumulated expertise, resilience, and contribution.**

Furthermore, the Scientific American article reveals something profound: our capacity for long life is not some fragile modern invention — it is **woven into our evolutionary heritage.** Research on SIGLEC genes shows that key genetic changes enhancing our immune defense occurred **200,000 to 100,000 years ago.** We are biologically built for longevity. Our bodies evolved powerful defense systems precisely to support longer lives. Dismissing this as something we are "not ready for" ignores hundreds of thousands of years of evolutionary preparation.

---

### 3. The "Mortality Plateau" — Our Biology Supports Us

The Futures Platform article cites a remarkable finding from researchers at **Sapienza University and the University of Roma**: after age 105, the risk of death **stops increasing.** They call it the "mortality plateau" — the odds of dying from one birthday to the next level off at roughly 50:50. This is revolutionary. It suggests that there may be no hard biological ceiling on human life, and that our bodies are more resilient at extreme ages than anyone previously believed.

This finding directly challenges the panelist from the model debate who argued that biology itself sets limits we cannot overcome. The data says otherwise. If anything, **our biology is more ready for longer life than our imaginations are.**

---

### 4. The Fear of Economic Collapse Is Overblown

Now, let me address the economic argument head-on, because I know my opponents will rely heavily on it. Yes, pension systems face pressure. Yes, retirement savings are insufficient for many. But these are **policy problems, not existential ones** — and we have solved far harder problems before.

The Futures Platform reading notes that **child mortality dropped from 43% in 1800 to 4.3% in 2015.** That did not happen by accident — it required massive investment in public health, education, and infrastructure. We built entirely new systems to accommodate the fact that children were surviving. We can — and will — do the same for longer-lived adults.

The real question is not "Can we afford longer lives?" but **"Can we afford to waste the potential of longer lives?"** If people live to 90 or 100 in good health, and we keep forcing them to retire at 65, then yes, the math doesn't work. But the answer is not to wish for shorter lives — it is to **redesign our institutions.** Countries like Japan, Singapore, and the Nordic nations are already pioneering flexible retirement, lifelong learning, and multi-generational workforce models. Readiness is not a fixed state — it is a process, and the process is well underway.

---

### 5. The Moral Case For Longer Lives

The panelist from the model debate who argued for the motion raised the issue of global inequality — and I take this argument seriously. It is true that life expectancy in sub-Saharan Africa lags behind developed nations. But the conclusion my opponents draw — that we should therefore slow down longevity progress — is **morally backwards.**

The Futures Platform article documents that the world is **getting safer** — homicide rates, conflict deaths, famine deaths, and maternal mortality have all been declining for decades. Child mortality has plummeted globally. **The same forces that extend life in wealthy nations are spreading to developing ones.** Between 1960 and 2015, global child mortality fell from 18.5% to 4.3% — and that improvement was not limited to the West. Longevity gains are diffusing, not concentrating.

Pijnenburg and Leget's justice argument — that life extension is morally indefensible while inequality exists — proves too much. By that logic, we should halt cancer research, stop developing vaccines, and abandon medical progress until every country reaches the same baseline. That is not justice — that is nihilism. The moral imperative is to **extend longevity gains to everyone**, not to deny them to anyone.

---

### 6. People Are More Ready Than They Think

Finally, let me address the Pew Research data that my opponents will cite. Yes, 56% of Americans say they would not want to live to 120. But look deeper: the median ideal lifespan Americans desire is **90 years** — higher than the current average of 78.7. People *do* want longer lives. They just want them to be **good lives.** And 63% of Americans believe that medical advances prolonging life are generally a positive development. The public is not rejecting longevity — they are demanding that it come with quality. That is not unreadiness — that is wisdom.

And consider this: **88% of Americans view population aging positively** — 41% as a good thing and 47% as neutral. Only 12% see it negatively. The narrative of fear and resistance simply does not match the data.

---

### Conclusion

Let me close with a perspective from the Scientific American reading. Our species did not achieve its remarkable longevity by being timid. Our ancestors evolved immune systems of extraordinary power, survived ice ages, crossed continents, and built civilizations — all while life expectancy was a fraction of what it is today. The idea that *this* generation, with all its technology, knowledge, and resources, is somehow "not ready" for a few more years of life is not just pessimistic — it is **historically absurd.**

Every era has its prophets of doom who insist we have reached our limits. And every era proves them wrong — not because change is easy, but because **human beings adapt.** That is what we do. That is what we have always done. That is what we will continue to do.

The Futures Platform article concludes that the future of human longevity is not predetermined — it depends on human effort. I say: let us make that effort. Let us meet the challenge of longer lives not with fear, but with the same ingenuity that brought us from an average lifespan of 35 to an average lifespan of 80 in just two centuries.

People are ready. People have always been ready. Vote against this motion. Thank you.
