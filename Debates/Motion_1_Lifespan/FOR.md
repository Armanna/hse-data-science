# FOR the Motion: "People Are Not Ready to Have a Longer Lifespan"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

Imagine this: you are 95 years old. Your savings ran out a decade ago. Your body is frail, your mind is fading, and the government that promised you a pension is itself bankrupt. You are alive — but you are not living. **This** is the future that longer lifespans promise if we remain as unprepared as we are today. I stand firmly in support of the motion: **people are not ready to have a longer lifespan** — not individually, not economically, not socially, and not psychologically.

Let me be clear: I am not arguing against the value of life. I am arguing that **extending life without extending our readiness for it is not a gift — it is a sentence.**

---

### 1. The Economic Time Bomb

My first argument is economic. The data is devastating. The Futures Platform article cites a study by the **World Economic Forum** showing that people should expect to outlive their retirement savings by **eight to twenty years on average**, with the heaviest burden falling on women. Think about that — not months, not a year, but up to two decades of life with no financial safety net.

This is not a hypothetical scenario. The **OECD projects** that age-related public spending could increase by 6.5 percentage points of GDP between 2021 and 2060 across member countries. The working-age population is estimated to fall by 13% over the next 40 years, dragging GDP per capita down by 14% by 2060. We are building a world where there are more mouths to feed and fewer hands to feed them. Already today, **80% of American households with older adults — 47 million people — are financially struggling or at risk** of economic insecurity as they age, according to the National Council on Aging.

So when Stanford biologist **Shripad Tuljapurkar** tells us — as cited in the Futures Platform reading — that people can expect to live roughly six years longer than their grandparents, we must ask: **who is paying for those six years?** The answer, right now, is nobody. Our pension systems, our savings habits, our entire economic architecture was designed for people who worked until 65 and died at 75. We are not ready.

---

### 2. Quality Versus Quantity — The Health Span Crisis

My second argument goes to the heart of what a "longer life" actually means. The panelist from the model debate who argued against the motion made an appealing case — that medical progress is a triumph. But this argument commits a fundamental error: it **confuses lifespan with health span**.

The PMC article "Three Arguments Against Extending the Human Lifespan" by **Pijnenburg and Leget** puts it powerfully: **"Quality of time outweighs quantity of time."** What good is living to 120 if you spend the last 30 years battling chronic disease, dementia, or immobility? The data from the National Council on Aging confirms that someone turning 65 today has almost a **70% chance of needing long-term care** services in their lifetime. We are not extending vitality — we are extending dependency. Our medical systems can keep bodies alive, but they cannot keep them thriving.

The communitarian argument from Pijnenburg and Leget further deepens this point: life has value only as **life lived in community**, in meaningful relationships. An extra decade spent isolated in a care facility, disconnected from society, is not a longer life in any meaningful sense — it is a longer death.

---

### 3. Society Says It Is Not Ready — And the Data Proves It

My third argument is perhaps the most straightforward: **people themselves are telling us they are not ready.** The Pew Research Center's comprehensive survey found that **56% of Americans would not choose medical treatments** to live to 120 or beyond. A full **51% believe radical life extension would be bad for society.** And the median ideal lifespan Americans desire? Not 120, not 150 — **just 90 years.** This is barely above current averages.

When **58% of people** consider extreme longevity "fundamentally unnatural" and **66% believe** such treatments would only be available to the wealthy — deepening inequality rather than solving it — we have a clear democratic verdict. People are looking at longer lifespans and saying: "No, thank you. Not like this."

Even the panelist from the model debate who was arguing for the motion acknowledged that increased longevity raises uncomfortable questions about inequality. And inequality is not a side effect — it is the central issue.

---

### 4. The Justice Argument — A Moral Reckoning

This brings me to my fourth and most powerful argument: justice. Pijnenburg and Leget, in the PMC article, call this the **strongest** of their three arguments against life extension, and I agree. While researchers in wealthy nations pursue the dream of living to 150, **life expectancy in sub-Saharan Africa remains below 60.** While we debate whether to live to 120, 25 million people in that region live with HIV.

The authors argue — and I find this devastating — that pursuing life extension research is morally indefensible when such profound inequality in basic lifespan exists. As they put it, this is a question of **"unequal death."** We cannot in good conscience pour billions into adding years to the lives of those who already live the longest while millions die young from preventable causes. **We are not ready for longer lifespans because we have not yet earned the moral right to pursue them.**

The utilitarian counterargument — that we should not deny good things to some because we cannot give them to all — misses the point entirely. It is not about denying a good; it is about **priorities**. A world that extends the lives of the privileged while abandoning the vulnerable is not a world that is ready for longer lifespans. It is a world that has lost its moral compass.

---

### 5. Engaging With the Opposition

Now, I anticipate my opponents will point to the Stanford data — that lifespans are increasing steadily, that older workers take fewer sick days, that the world is getting safer. And these facts are true. But **the fact that something is happening does not mean we are ready for it.** Climate change is happening. Artificial intelligence is reshaping labor markets. These are realities — and we are manifestly not ready for them either.

The panelist who argued against the motion in the model debate suggested that human ingenuity will solve these problems. But hope is not a plan. Where are the reformed pension systems? Where are the healthcare models designed for populations where 30% are over 65? Where are the social structures that give meaning and purpose to people in their ninth, tenth, or eleventh decade of life? They do not exist. **We are sprinting toward a longer future with no map, no provisions, and no destination.**

---

### Conclusion

Let me leave you with this thought from the spiritual and philosophical traditions surveyed by Pijnenburg and Leget: across Christianity, Islam, Judaism, Hinduism, and Buddhism, wisdom traditions converge on one insight — that the meaning of life lies not in its **duration** but in its **depth.** Not in how long we live, but in how fully, how generously, how meaningfully.

People are not ready for longer lifespans. Our economies cannot fund them. Our healthcare systems cannot sustain them. Our social institutions cannot support them. Our moral priorities do not justify them. And — most tellingly — **our own people do not want them.**

I urge you to support this motion. Thank you.
