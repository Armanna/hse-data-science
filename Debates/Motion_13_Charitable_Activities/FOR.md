# FOR the Motion: "Most People Engage in Charitable Activities Because They Are More Concerned with Feeling Good About Themselves Than Actually Doing Good"

## Speech (~7-8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

Here is an uncomfortable truth that the philanthropic world does not want you to hear: **charity, as most people practice it, is primarily an act of self-service dressed up as selflessness.** The warm glow, the social approval, the tax deduction, the Instagram post — these are not side effects of generosity. They are its primary drivers. I stand firmly in support of this motion: **most people engage in charitable activities because they are more concerned with feeling good about themselves than actually doing good.**

Let me be direct: **if donors truly cared about impact above all else, the charitable landscape would look radically different from what it does today.** The fact that it doesn't tells us everything we need to know about what really motivates giving.

---

### 1. The Science of "Warm Glow" — Charity Activates the Self-Reward System

My first argument draws on the extensive psychological research on what economists call **"warm glow giving,"** a concept introduced by economist **James Andreoni.** Warm glow theory holds that people donate not purely to improve outcomes for recipients, but because the act of giving itself produces a pleasurable emotional reward — a sense of moral satisfaction, a feeling of being a good person.

This is not speculation. A landmark **natural field experiment** on charitable giving found that individuals who received appeals emphasizing **benefits to themselves** (the warm glow) were **6.6% more likely to give and donated 23% more** than those in a control group. By contrast, messages that appealed to **benefits for recipients** — the supposedly altruistic motivation — had **no statistically significant effect** on average donations.

Let that sink in. When you tell people "your donation will help others," it barely moves them. When you tell people "giving will make you feel good," they open their wallets. The research is clear: **approximately 50% of donors are significantly motivated by warm glow** — by looking good to themselves (self-image) and to others (social image). The charitable impulse, for most people, is fundamentally about the donor, not the recipient.

---

### 2. The Philanthropy Industry Serves Donors, Not Beneficiaries

My second argument draws on the critique of big philanthropy articulated in the **Johnson Center's** analysis of increasing critiques of the philanthropic sector. The authors document a structural reality: **the entire architecture of modern philanthropy is designed to serve donor preferences, not recipient needs.**

Consider **Donor-Advised Funds (DAFs)**, which the Johnson Center highlights as a growing concern. DAFs allow wealthy individuals to claim **immediate tax deductions** while retaining indefinite control over when — or whether — the money actually reaches charitable causes. There is **no mandatory payout requirement and no public reporting requirement.** Donors get the tax benefit and the warm glow of "giving" without any obligation to actually distribute the funds. As **Ray Madoff** has argued, DAFs create a **moral hazard** — the appearance of generosity without the substance.

The Johnson Center further notes that **economic inequality fuels philanthropic inequality** — charitable dollars are increasingly concentrated among fewer large donors who direct funds toward elite causes: opera houses, university buildings bearing their names, art museums. As **Anand Giridharadas** argues in *Winners Take All*, elite philanthropy is often an **"elite charade of changing the world"** — a mechanism through which the wealthy maintain their status and influence while appearing magnanimous. The system rewards donors with tax breaks, social prestige, and political access. **If this were truly about doing good, the incentive structure would prioritize impact, not donor satisfaction.**

---

### 3. Effective Altruism's Own Existence Proves the Motion

My third argument is perhaps the most ironic: the very existence of the **Effective Altruism (EA) movement** is itself evidence that most charitable giving is not about doing good. The EA movement, as described on its own website, was founded on the insight that **"some charities help 100 or even 1,000 times as many people as others"** when given equivalent resources. EA exists precisely because the vast majority of donors do not care about effectiveness.

If most people were genuinely motivated by doing good, they would naturally seek out the most effective interventions. They would give to **insecticide-treated bednets** that save lives for approximately **$5,500 each** — roughly 180 times cheaper than wealthy-nation medical interventions. They would prioritize **pandemic prevention**, which receives only **$8 billion annually** compared to $280 billion for counterterrorism, despite pandemics causing far more deaths historically.

But they don't. Instead, donors give to causes that are emotionally compelling, personally familiar, or socially prestigious. They donate to their alma mater, their local church, disaster relief campaigns that trend on social media — not because these are the most effective uses of charitable dollars, but because these donations produce the strongest warm glow. **The EA movement is a correction to a charitable system driven by feelings, not outcomes — and its marginal status proves how few donors actually prioritize doing good.**

---

### 4. Philanthropy as Power and Status — The Saunders-Hastings Critique

My fourth argument draws on the work of political philosopher **Emma Saunders-Hastings**, whose research on philanthropy and democratic equality was featured in *The New Yorker.* Saunders-Hastings argues that philanthropy produces **"objectionably hierarchical social and political relationships"** — that **"some people's altruism puts other people under their power."**

This is a crucial insight. When a billionaire donates to a university, a hospital, or a political cause, the donation is not a neutral transfer of resources. It is an exercise of power — the power to shape institutions, influence policy, and define social priorities according to the donor's preferences, not the public's needs. Saunders-Hastings identifies the fundamental problem: even when philanthropy improves material conditions, it is often practiced through **"conditioned gifts and legal arrangements that privilege donor control"** in ways that undermine democratic equality.

This is not altruism. This is **influence purchasing with a charitable tax deduction.** The donor feels good. The donor's name goes on a building. The donor gains social capital and political access. The recipients, meanwhile, have no voice in how the money is spent, no accountability mechanism, and no democratic recourse. **When charity reinforces the power of the giver over the receiver, it is self-serving by definition — regardless of the material benefits it produces.**

---

### 5. The Behavioral Evidence — People Give When It Feels Good, Not When It Does Good

My fifth argument synthesizes the behavioral research on charitable motivations. Studies consistently identify **six core motives for giving**: trust, altruism, social benefits, tax benefits, egoism (reputation enhancement and positive self-regard), and emotional satisfaction. Notice that only one of these six — altruism — is genuinely other-oriented.

The research reveals that **reputation and signaling** are powerful drivers: philanthropy communicates wealth, status, and desirable personal traits. Donors sometimes give to **restore a threatened group identity, improve their group image, or demonstrate their group's power.** Corporate philanthropy, meanwhile, is often explicitly strategic — designed to improve brand perception, attract customers, and generate goodwill.

Even individual donors behave in ways that reveal self-interested motivations. People donate more when their giving is **publicly visible.** They give more to causes with **emotional narratives** — a single identifiable victim — than to statistical evidence of where money would do the most good. They give more during the **holiday season**, when social pressure and personal guilt peak, regardless of when donations would be most effective.

**Peter Singer** posed a famous question: when hundreds of millions of dollars flowed to rebuild Notre-Dame Cathedral after the 2019 fire, how many lives could those same dollars have saved? The answer reveals what donors truly value: **monuments to their own culture and identity, not the lives of strangers.**

---

### Conclusion

Let me conclude by addressing the obvious objection: "But people do give, and that giving does help others. Doesn't that matter?" Of course the material effects of charity matter. I am not arguing that charitable activities produce no good. I am arguing that **the primary motivation for most donors is not the good produced — it is the feeling experienced.**

The warm glow research proves it: appeals to self-interest move donors; appeals to recipient benefit do not. The structure of the philanthropic industry proves it: DAFs, naming rights, tax deductions, and perpetual endowments all serve donors first. The Effective Altruism movement proves it by its very existence: if people cared about impact, EA would be the default, not the exception. Emma Saunders-Hastings proves it: philanthropy creates hierarchical relationships that serve the powerful.

**Charity, as practiced by most people, is a transaction in which the primary product is not social good — it is personal satisfaction.** The warm glow is not a bonus. It is the point.

I urge you to support this motion — not because generosity is worthless, but because honesty about our motivations is the first step toward a charitable system that truly prioritizes doing good. Thank you.
