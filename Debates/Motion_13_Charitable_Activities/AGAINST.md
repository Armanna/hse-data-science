# AGAINST the Motion: "Most People Engage in Charitable Activities Because They Are More Concerned with Feeling Good About Themselves Than Actually Doing Good"

## Speech (~7-8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

This motion asks you to believe something deeply cynical: that the billions of dollars donated annually, the millions of volunteer hours contributed, the countless acts of quiet generosity performed by ordinary people around the world — all of this is fundamentally selfish. That the primary engine of human charity is vanity, not compassion. **I reject this motion entirely.** It is built on a misreading of psychological research, a conflation of elite philanthropy with everyday giving, and a profoundly reductive view of human motivation.

Let me state my position clearly: **the fact that giving feels good does not mean that feeling good is the reason people give. Confusing the emotional byproduct of generosity with its cause is the central logical error of this motion.**

---

### 1. The Warm Glow Argument Proves Too Much — and Too Little

My first argument directly challenges the cornerstone of the proposition's case: the **warm glow theory** associated with economist **James Andreoni.** Yes, research confirms that giving activates reward centers in the brain. Yes, donors experience positive emotions when they give. The proposition will cite this as proof that charity is self-serving.

But this argument proves too much. By the same logic, **every human activity that produces satisfaction is "selfish."** A parent who enjoys raising their children is selfish. A doctor who feels fulfilled saving lives is selfish. A firefighter who experiences pride in rescuing people from a burning building is selfish. If warm glow disqualifies altruism, then altruism is logically impossible — and the concept becomes meaningless.

The research itself is more nuanced than the proposition admits. Studies distinguish between **self-image warm glow** (feeling good about yourself) and **other-oriented satisfaction** (feeling good because someone else benefited). The fact that these overlap does not mean they are identical. When a donor gives to a malaria prevention charity and feels satisfied knowing that a child will survive, **the satisfaction is caused by the good done — not the other way around.** The warm glow is a consequence of caring about others, not a substitute for it.

Furthermore, research from the **University of Sussex** published in 2018 found that **the warm glow of kindness is real even when there is nothing material in it for the giver** — challenging the claim that self-interest is the primary driver. People experience satisfaction from anonymous giving, from helping strangers they will never meet, and from acts of charity that carry no social reward whatsoever.

---

### 2. Everyday Giving Is Invisible — and That Refutes the Self-Interest Thesis

My second argument moves from theory to evidence. If most charitable activity were driven by self-interest, status signaling, and social approval, then we would expect charitable behavior to be overwhelmingly **public, visible, and performative.** But the reality is precisely the opposite.

The vast majority of charitable activity worldwide is **anonymous, small-scale, and completely invisible.** Millions of people donate modest amounts to food banks, religious institutions, disaster relief funds, and community organizations without any public recognition, social media posts, or naming rights. They volunteer at homeless shelters, mentor disadvantaged youth, and care for elderly neighbors. None of this generates status, tax advantages, or social capital.

The proposition's argument relies heavily on critiques of **elite philanthropy** — billionaire foundations, Donor-Advised Funds, naming rights on buildings. But elite philanthropy represents a tiny fraction of total charitable activity. **Ordinary people giving $20 to a food bank or spending Saturday mornings at a community kitchen are not seeking power, influence, or tax optimization. They are responding to a basic human impulse: the desire to help.**

The Johnson Center's own analysis, which the proposition cites, acknowledges that criticism of philanthropy is primarily directed at **"big philanthropy"** — the concentrated wealth of major donors. Extrapolating from the behavior of billionaires to the motivations of ordinary people is a fundamental category error.

---

### 3. Effective Altruism Demonstrates Genuine Concern for Doing Good

My third argument turns the proposition's own evidence against them. The proposition will argue that the Effective Altruism movement proves most giving is self-serving because EA exists to correct donors' failure to prioritize impact. But this interpretation is backwards.

The **EA movement**, as described in its own introduction, is founded on four core values: **scope sensitivity** (caring about helping more people rather than fewer), **impartiality** (helping those with the greatest need regardless of proximity), **scout mindset** (pursuing truth rather than defending existing beliefs), and **tradeoff recognition** (acknowledging opportunity costs). These are not the values of a self-interested movement. These are the values of people **genuinely committed to doing the most good possible.**

EA demonstrates that a significant and growing community of people are willing to **redirect their charitable dollars away from emotionally comfortable causes toward maximally effective interventions** — even when this means giving to unglamorous causes in distant countries. EA members pledge significant portions of their income, choose careers based on social impact rather than salary, and subject their own giving to rigorous evidence-based scrutiny.

Now, **Berger and Penna**, writing in the *Stanford Social Innovation Review*, criticize EA as **"elitist"** and **"charitable imperialism"** for ranking causes against one another. They object to Peter Singer's comparison of providing a guide dog to one American versus curing blindness in 2,000 people overseas. But notice what Berger and Penna are actually defending: they argue for **"giving with their heads as well as their hearts"** — for informed donors supporting causes aligned with personal values. Even EA's critics do not claim that donors are primarily self-interested. They argue about the **method** of doing good, not about whether donors genuinely want to do good. **The entire debate within the charitable sector is about how best to help — which presupposes that the desire to help is real.**

---

### 4. The Structural Critique Confuses Institutions with Individuals

My fourth argument addresses the institutional critiques raised by **Emma Saunders-Hastings** and **Anand Giridharadas.** Saunders-Hastings argues that philanthropy creates **"objectionably hierarchical social and political relationships"** and that donor power can undermine democratic equality. Giridharadas calls elite philanthropy an **"elite charade."**

These are important critiques of **how philanthropic institutions are structured** — and I take them seriously. But they do not prove what this motion claims. The motion is about **why most people engage in charitable activities.** Saunders-Hastings' critique is about the **political consequences** of philanthropic power, not about the **psychological motivations** of individual donors. A middle-class family donating to their local hospital may be contributing to a system that Saunders-Hastings would critique — but that says nothing about whether the family's motivation is self-interested or genuinely altruistic.

Moreover, even the strongest critics of philanthropy do not argue for its abolition. The Johnson Center explicitly advocates for **research-informed, constructive critique** aimed at improving philanthropy, not eliminating it. **Rob Reich**, author of *Just Giving: Why Philanthropy Is Failing Democracy*, argues that the problem is not that donors are insincere — it is that the **tax and legal structures** surrounding philanthropy distort outcomes. The donors' motivations may be genuine even when the system's effects are problematic. **Conflating systemic critique with motivational cynicism is intellectually dishonest.**

---

### 5. Human Psychology Is Complex — The Binary of "Selfish vs. Selfless" Is False

My fifth argument challenges the fundamental framing of this motion. The motion forces a binary: people either care about doing good or they care about feeling good. But **human motivation does not work this way.** Research consistently shows that prosocial behavior involves a **plurality of motives** — and that the coexistence of self-oriented and other-oriented motivations does not invalidate either.

Yes, donors experience warm glow. Yes, some donors value social recognition. Yes, tax benefits influence giving behavior. But research also shows that **self-reported altruists behave measurably differently from self-reported warm-glow givers**: altruists are significantly more likely to pay to increase the probability that their donation will be effectively implemented, and they give more on average. This demonstrates that a substantial portion of donors are **genuinely motivated by outcomes for recipients**, not merely by their own emotional satisfaction.

The research further reveals something counterintuitive: **self-benefiting incentives can actually crowd out intrinsic motivations to give.** When you emphasize the personal benefits of charity — the tax break, the status signal — it can reduce genuine charitable motivation. This suggests that the intrinsic desire to help is real and psychologically distinct from self-interest. If charity were truly just about feeling good, then emphasizing personal benefits should always increase giving. The fact that it sometimes **decreases** giving proves that something deeper is at work: **a genuine moral commitment to others that resists being reduced to self-interest.**

---

### Conclusion

Let me conclude by asking a simple question: if most charitable activity is primarily self-serving, how do we explain the millions of people who give **anonymously**, who volunteer in **thankless roles**, who donate to causes that will **never benefit them personally**, and who continue giving even when it brings **no social recognition, no tax advantage, and no warm glow sufficient to justify the sacrifice?**

The proposition's argument rests on a selective reading of research that conflates correlation with causation. Yes, giving produces positive emotions. That does not mean positive emotions are the purpose of giving. The warm glow is not the motive — **it is the reward that evolution built into our psychology to reinforce genuinely prosocial behavior.**

James Andreoni's warm glow theory describes a mechanism, not a motivation. Emma Saunders-Hastings critiques institutions, not individual hearts. The Effective Altruism movement demonstrates that millions of people care enough about doing good to subject their giving to rigorous scrutiny. And the vast, invisible ocean of everyday charity — the food bank donations, the volunteer hours, the quiet acts of generosity that no one will ever see — refutes the cynicism of this motion more powerfully than any academic study could.

**Most people give because they care. The feeling good is a bonus, not a business plan.**

I urge you to vote against this motion and to resist the temptation of fashionable cynicism about human nature. Thank you.
