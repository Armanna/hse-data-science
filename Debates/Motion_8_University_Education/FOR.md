# FOR the Motion: "More people should be able to access university education"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

In 1960, only **13% of the world's young adults** had the opportunity to attend university. Today, that number has reached **40%** — a transformation that represents the single greatest democratization of knowledge in human history. Yet despite this progress, **UNESCO** reports that in low-income countries, the tertiary enrollment rate remains stuck at just **11%**, while in high-income countries it exceeds **75%**. This is not a technological barrier. This is not a question of capacity. This is a question of **access** — and access is a choice.

I stand firmly in support of this motion: **more people should be able to access university education.** Not because everyone must attend university, but because everyone who can benefit from it **should have the opportunity** to do so. And the evidence shows that expanding that opportunity creates benefits that ripple through individuals, economies, and entire societies.

---

### 1. University Education Delivers Measurable Economic Returns

My first argument is about economics — and the numbers are overwhelming.

According to **American RadioWorks**, college graduates earn on average **84% more** than those with only a high school diploma over their lifetimes. The **New York Times**, analyzing 2024 data, reports that the median income for workers with bachelor's degrees is **$2.8 million** over a career, compared to **$1.6 million** for high school graduates. That is a **$1.2 million lifetime earnings premium** — a premium that has remained remarkably stable even as college enrollment has expanded.

But the economic case for university access goes far beyond individual earnings. **The Economist** reports that tertiary education is **"more important than ever for young people"** precisely because the modern knowledge economy rewards cognitive skills, adaptability, and specialized expertise — skills that university education systematically develops. Countries with higher tertiary enrollment rates show higher GDP per capita, greater innovation capacity, and more resilient labor markets.

And here is the critical point: when we expand access to university education, we do not dilute these benefits — we **multiply** them. UNESCO's 2022 Global Data Report shows that countries that have dramatically expanded higher education access — South Korea, Singapore, Ireland — have simultaneously seen explosive economic growth. Education is not a zero-sum game where more graduates mean less value per degree. It is a **positive-sum investment** where expanding access raises productivity across the entire economy.

The panelist from the model debate who argues against the motion will likely claim that not everyone benefits from university. But that argument confuses "not everyone must attend" with "fewer people should have access." These are not the same. Expanding access means expanding **opportunity** — letting individuals make informed choices about their own futures, rather than having those choices foreclosed by accident of birth.

---

### 2. University Access Is a Social Mobility Engine

My second argument addresses what may be the most powerful justification for expanding university access: its role in breaking cycles of poverty and inequality.

**The Economist** reports that university education is the single strongest predictor of upward social mobility across developed economies. Children from low-income families who complete university degrees earn, on average, **three times more** than their parents. For children from wealthy families, the premium is much smaller — because they already have access to opportunity. University education does not just create economic value; it **redistributes opportunity** toward those who need it most.

UNESCO's data confirms this globally. In countries where tertiary enrollment has expanded to include first-generation college students — students whose parents never attended university — income inequality has **narrowed**, not widened. Brazil's expansion of public university access in the 2000s lifted millions into the middle class. India's network of public universities, despite resource constraints, has created the world's largest cohort of university-educated professionals, many from families that had been locked out of opportunity for generations.

The alternative — restricting university access to those who can afford it or who happen to be born into educated families — is not neutral. It is a **perpetuation of inequality.** When we say "more people should be able to access university education," we are saying that talent and ambition, not family wealth, should determine who gets the opportunity to develop their potential.

---

### 3. The Knowledge Economy Demands Higher Education at Scale

My third argument confronts the economic transformation that makes university access not just beneficial but **necessary** at a scale we have never seen before.

**The Economist** documents the shift: in 1980, manufacturing and manual labor dominated employment across developed economies. Today, the fastest-growing job categories are **knowledge-intensive** — data analysis, healthcare, engineering, education, creative industries. These are jobs that require university-level training, not because of credentialism, but because the cognitive demands of the work genuinely require advanced education.

The **New York Times** reports that **65% of jobs** in high-income economies now require post-secondary education, up from just **28% in 1973.** This is not a temporary trend. It is a structural transformation driven by technology, globalization, and the increasing complexity of modern economies. Artificial intelligence, biotechnology, renewable energy, digital infrastructure — the industries shaping the 21st century all require workforces with advanced training.

And here is the uncomfortable truth: when university access is restricted, economies **cannot fill these roles.** The **UNESCO Global Data Report** warns that developing countries face a **skills crisis** — not because their populations lack talent, but because their education systems cannot provide access at the scale the modern economy demands. Nigeria has 200 million people and a tertiary enrollment rate of **10%.** India, despite massive expansion, still enrolls only **28%** of its young adults in higher education. These are not sustainable numbers for economies trying to compete globally.

Expanding university access is not about forcing everyone into the same path. It is about ensuring that economies have the skilled workforces they need — and that individuals have the training to access the jobs that actually exist, not the jobs that existed in 1975.

---

### 4. University Education Develops Critical Citizens, Not Just Workers

My fourth argument moves beyond economics to the civic and social benefits of university education — benefits that the market cannot measure but that democratic societies depend upon.

**American RadioWorks** reports that university graduates vote at **significantly higher rates** than non-graduates, engage more frequently in community organizations, and demonstrate higher levels of civic knowledge. **The Economist** notes that countries with higher tertiary enrollment show stronger democratic institutions, lower corruption, and greater public trust in government.

Why? Because university education does not just train workers — it trains **critical thinkers.** Students learn to evaluate evidence, challenge assumptions, engage with complexity, and understand perspectives different from their own. These are not job skills. These are **citizenship skills** — and they are essential to functional democracies.

The UNESCO report emphasizes this explicitly: higher education is a **"global public good"** because it produces citizens capable of addressing complex global challenges — climate change, public health, technological governance, inequality. When we restrict university access, we restrict the number of people equipped to engage meaningfully with the defining questions of our time.

The panelist arguing against the motion may claim that vocational training can deliver the same benefits. But vocational training, as valuable as it is, does not systematically teach students to question power, analyze policy, or engage with abstract ideas. University education does. And societies that want informed, engaged citizens — not just productive workers — need to make that education accessible.

---

### 5. Expanding Access Does Not Mean Lowering Standards

My fifth argument addresses the most common objection to expanding university access: the claim that more access means lower quality.

This argument is empirically wrong. **The Economist** documents that countries like South Korea, which expanded tertiary enrollment from **16% in 1980 to over 70% today,** did not see a collapse in educational quality. Instead, they saw the emergence of world-class universities, higher average skill levels across the workforce, and explosive economic growth. **Ireland** expanded university access dramatically in the 1990s while simultaneously rising in international education rankings.

The confusion stems from conflating **access** with **outcomes.** Expanding access means removing barriers — financial, geographic, social — that prevent qualified students from attending. It does not mean admitting unqualified students or eliminating academic standards. UNESCO's data shows that countries with high tertiary enrollment rates maintain rigorous graduation requirements — but they provide support systems (financial aid, tutoring, flexible schedules) that allow students to meet those requirements.

The **New York Times** notes that the return on investment for a university degree has remained **remarkably stable** even as enrollment has expanded — strong evidence that degree quality has not been diluted. If degrees were becoming less valuable, the earnings premium would shrink. It has not.

Expanding access is not about lowering the bar. It is about ensuring that everyone capable of clearing the bar **gets the chance to try.** And when we expand that opportunity, we discover talent that restrictive systems overlook — first-generation students, rural students, students from underrepresented communities who perform just as well as their peers when given the resources to succeed.

---

### Conclusion

Let me close with a simple question: who decides who gets the opportunity for a university education?

In much of the world today, that decision is made by **accident of birth** — by whether you were born into a family that can afford tuition, born in a country with accessible public universities, born into a social network that expects higher education. Talent is distributed randomly. Opportunity is not.

The evidence is overwhelming. University graduates earn **$1.2 million more** over their lifetimes. Countries with higher tertiary enrollment show stronger economic growth, lower inequality, and healthier democracies. The modern knowledge economy **requires** advanced education at scale. And expanding access — when done with adequate support — does not lower quality; it raises the total pool of human capital.

The motion does not say everyone **must** attend university. It says more people **should be able to access** it. Access is about removing barriers. Access is about expanding opportunity. Access is about letting talent, not privilege, determine who gets the chance to develop their potential.

I urge you to support this motion. Thank you.
