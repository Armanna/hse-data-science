# AGAINST the Motion: "More people should be able to access university education"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

In the United States, **43 million Americans** carry student loan debt totaling **$1.7 trillion.** That is more than the GDP of Canada. And for what? **NPR** reports that while young people line up for university degrees, high-paying skilled trade jobs — electricians, plumbers, welders — sit **empty** across the economy. In 2018, there were **30,000 unfilled welding jobs** in the U.S. alone, with **hundreds of thousands more** needed by 2026. These jobs pay **$50,000 to $90,000** annually, require no student debt, and cannot be outsourced.

Meanwhile, **The New York Times** reports that **52% of recent college graduates** are working in jobs that **do not require a degree.** They have the debt. They have the credential. But they do not have the career the credential was supposed to unlock.

I stand firmly against this motion. Not because education is not valuable — it is — but because the push to expand university access has become **disconnected from reality.** We have created a system that tells every young person they **must** attend university to succeed, while simultaneously devaluing vocational skills, burying students in debt, and producing graduates for jobs that do not exist.

More people do not need access to university education. More people need access to **the right education for their talents, goals, and the actual economy they will enter.** And for millions, that education is not university — it is something we have systematically failed to value.

---

### 1. We Have Created a Credential Inflation Crisis

My first argument is about supply and demand — and the devastating consequences of ignoring economic reality.

**The Economist** reports that in the United States and across much of the developed world, we are producing **far more university graduates than the economy has university-level jobs to offer.** The result is not increased prosperity — it is **credential inflation.** Jobs that once required a high school diploma now demand a bachelor's degree. Jobs that once required a bachelor's now demand a master's. Not because the work has become more complex, but because employers use degrees as a **screening mechanism** when too many applicants hold the same credential.

**The New York Times** quantifies the problem: **52% of recent college graduates** work in jobs that do not require a degree. These are not temporary transitions — they are long-term outcomes. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York reports that **41% of all college graduates** are underemployed, meaning they work in positions that do not utilize their education. A psychology degree to work retail. An English degree to answer phones. A sociology degree to serve coffee.

This is not a failure of individual students. This is a **systemic failure** of a higher education model that has expanded access without expanding the jobs that justify that access. The FOR side will celebrate enrollment numbers climbing to 40% globally. I ask: what happens when 60% of young people have degrees but only 30% of jobs require them? You do not get more opportunity. You get a **more expensive sorting mechanism** that leaves millions in debt and underemployed.

The panelist arguing for the motion will claim that education is valuable beyond employment. And they are right. But when that education costs **tens of thousands of dollars** and four years of opportunity cost, we cannot ignore the economic reality that it is **not delivering on its promise** for a huge proportion of students.

---

### 2. We Have Abandoned Vocational Education — With Catastrophic Results

My second argument confronts the most damaging consequence of university-obsessed education policy: the systematic **devaluing of skilled trades.**

**NPR** documents the crisis with devastating clarity. Across the United States, industries face **critical shortages** of welders, electricians, plumbers, HVAC technicians, and other skilled tradespeople. In Montana, **67% of contractors** cannot find qualified workers. In South Carolina, manufacturers report **65,000 unfilled skilled positions.** These are not minimum-wage jobs. These are careers paying **$50,000 to $90,000 annually** — often more than many jobs held by university graduates — with **zero student debt.**

And yet, when young people express interest in these paths, they are told they are **"settling."** Guidance counselors push university. Parents express disappointment. Society sends a clear message: if you do not attend university, you have failed.

The result? **Mike Rowe**, host of *Dirty Jobs* and advocate for skilled trades, reports that while **7 million jobs** sit unfilled in skilled trades, we have convinced an entire generation that these jobs are beneath them. We have created an economy where plumbers earn more than many accountants — but we tell young people to get accounting degrees and avoid trade school at all costs.

This is not just economically irrational. It is **socially destructive.** The FOR side argues that expanding university access promotes equality. But what equality is there in pushing students who have no interest in academic work, who would thrive in hands-on skilled trades, into university classrooms where they struggle, accumulate debt, and emerge four years later with a credential they did not need for the career they actually wanted?

**UNESCO's** own data shows that countries with strong vocational training systems — Germany, Switzerland, Austria — have **lower youth unemployment, higher productivity, and less education-related debt** than countries that prioritize universal university access. The lesson is clear: more university access is not the answer. **Better matched education pathways** are.

---

### 3. The Student Debt Crisis Is Crushing a Generation

My third argument addresses the single most tangible harm of the push for expanded university access: **student debt.**

In the United States, **43 million borrowers** owe a collective **$1.7 trillion** in student loan debt. The average borrower owes **$37,000** — and that is for a bachelor's degree. Graduate degrees push that number far higher. **The New York Times** reports that this debt delays homeownership, delays marriage, delays childbearing, and constrains career choices for decades.

And for what? As I have already shown, **over half of recent graduates** work in jobs that do not require their degree. They have paid the cost. They have carried the debt. But they have not received the promised return.

The FOR side will argue that the solution is to make university free or more affordable. But this misses the deeper problem: **we are sending people to university who should not be there** — not because they lack intelligence, but because university is not the right path for their goals. Making a bad investment cheaper does not make it a good investment. It just means more people make the bad investment.

**The Economist** notes that countries that have expanded university access without addressing labor market realities have created **cohorts of indebted, underemployed graduates.** Spain expanded tertiary enrollment dramatically in the 2000s — and now has **youth unemployment above 30%** and a generation of overqualified baristas. Italy faces similar outcomes. These are not success stories. They are **cautionary tales.**

The panelist for the motion will point to high-earning university graduates. But they will not mention the **millions** who graduate into debt, underemployment, and regret. When we expand access without expanding **outcomes**, we do not create opportunity — we create a **debt trap.**

---

### 4. Not Everyone Benefits from Academic Education

My fourth argument confronts an uncomfortable truth that the FOR side refuses to acknowledge: **university-style academic education is not suited to everyone** — and pretending it is does profound harm.

Human beings have different aptitudes. Some thrive in abstract, text-heavy, lecture-based environments. Others learn best by **doing** — through apprenticeships, hands-on training, direct application of skills. For the second group, forcing them into university classrooms is not expanding opportunity — it is **setting them up for failure.**

**NPR** interviews with young tradespeople reveal a consistent pattern: students who struggled in traditional academic settings, who were told they were not "college material," discover that they are **exceptional** at skilled trades. A welder who barely passed high school English becomes a master craftsperson earning **$80,000 a year.** An electrician who hated essay-writing finds profound satisfaction and financial security in a career that requires spatial reasoning and technical skill, not academic writing.

The FOR side treats university access as a universal good, as if everyone benefits equally. But **UNESCO's** own data shows that university dropout rates are **highest among students from non-academic backgrounds** — precisely the students that expanded access is supposed to help. We bring them in. We saddle them with debt. And then they leave without a degree, worse off than if they had never enrolled.

This is not kindness. This is not opportunity. This is a **one-size-fits-all model** applied to a population with radically diverse needs, talents, and goals. The motion assumes that more university access equals more opportunity. But for millions, the opportunity they need is **an alternative to university** — and we have systematically dismantled and devalued that alternative.

---

### 5. We Are Ignoring What the Economy Actually Needs

My fifth argument forces us to confront labor market reality: **the economy does not need more university graduates.** It needs the **right mix of skills** — and we are producing the wrong mix.

**The New York Times** reports that the fastest-growing job categories over the next decade include **healthcare support, personal care, food service, construction, and transportation** — categories that require **vocational training or associate degrees,** not four-year university education. Meanwhile, we continue to graduate tens of thousands of students in fields with **no job market** — humanities, social sciences, and liberal arts degrees that the FOR side celebrates as "critical thinking training" but that leave graduates unemployed or underemployed.

**The Economist** warns that this mismatch has real consequences. Companies cannot find skilled tradespeople, healthcare technicians, or practical engineers — so they raise wages, driving up costs across the economy. Meanwhile, **millions of university graduates compete for a limited number of knowledge-economy jobs,** driving wages down in those sectors. We have created **simultaneous shortage and surplus** — a catastrophically inefficient outcome.

Germany, Switzerland, and Austria offer a different model. These countries have **robust vocational training systems** that channel students into apprenticeships, technical education, and university based on aptitude and interest, not a blanket assumption that everyone benefits from university. The result? **Lower youth unemployment. Higher productivity. Better skills match.** And critically, **social respect for non-university pathways.**

The FOR side will argue that university education drives innovation and growth. But innovation requires the **right people** in university — those with academic aptitude and interest — not **everyone,** regardless of fit. Expanding access for its own sake does not create innovation. It creates **credential inflation, debt, and inefficiency.**

---

### Conclusion

Let me be absolutely clear about what I am **not** arguing.

I am not arguing that university education is bad. I am not arguing that no one should attend. I am not arguing against access for those who can genuinely benefit.

I am arguing that the motion — **"More people should be able to access university education"** — rests on a fundamentally flawed premise: that university is the **right path for more people** than currently have access.

The evidence says otherwise. **Fifty-two percent of recent graduates** work in jobs that do not require degrees. **Forty-three million Americans** carry **$1.7 trillion** in student debt. **Hundreds of thousands of high-paying skilled trade jobs** sit unfilled because we have convinced young people these jobs are beneath them. Countries that expanded university access without addressing labor market needs face **youth unemployment crises and underemployment epidemics.**

More people do not need access to university. More people need access to **education systems that match their talents to the economy's needs.** They need strong vocational training. They need respected apprenticeship systems. They need a society that values a master electrician as much as it values a sociology graduate.

The FOR side will show you enrollment numbers and earnings averages. I show you **millions of individuals** — buried in debt, working jobs they did not need degrees for, or unable to find work in their field — and ask: is this the success story we want to expand?

Vote against this motion. Thank you.
