# AGAINST the Motion: "The high prices set by pharmaceutical companies on drugs are justified"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

In 2015, a man named **Martin Shkreli** acquired the rights to a drug called Daraprim — a 62-year-old medication used to treat a life-threatening parasitic infection common in AIDS patients. Overnight, he raised the price by **5,000%.** A pill that had cost $13.50 suddenly cost $750. He did not improve the drug. He did not invest in new research. He simply realized that sick people had no alternative — and he charged them accordingly.

Martin Shkreli went to prison — but not for price gouging. In America, what he did to the price of Daraprim was **perfectly legal.** And that is the point. Shkreli was not an aberration. He was the logical endpoint of a system that grants pharmaceutical companies **unchecked monopoly power** over products that people need to survive.

I stand firmly against this motion. The high prices set by pharmaceutical companies are **not** justified. They are the product of monopoly power, patent manipulation, and a system designed to maximize shareholder returns — not to reward innovation or serve patients.

---

### 1. There Is No Relationship Between Price and R&D Cost

My first argument strikes at the heart of the industry's defense. Pharmaceutical companies claim that high prices reflect the enormous cost of research and development. The evidence shows this is simply **not true.**

**Els Torreele**, a researcher at University College London's Institute for Innovation and Public Purpose, states it plainly: **"Medicine prices are largely based on what companies can get away with, independent of production and R&D costs."** There is, she argues, **"no relation between cost and price"** — pricing is based on monopoly power, not on recovering investment.

Consider the evidence. **Imatinib** (branded as Gleevec), a breakthrough treatment for chronic myeloid leukemia, was approved in 2001 at a price of **$24,000 per year.** By 2016, the price had risen to **$100,000 per year** — a fourfold increase on a drug whose development costs had long since been recovered. **Lenalidomide**, used for multiple myeloma, increased from **$215 per pill in 2005 to $763 per pill in 2020.** As the *Nature* journal documented, these are not prices being set to recoup R&D — they are prices being **raised** on drugs that are already wildly profitable because patients have no alternative.

The panelist from the model debate who argues for the motion will present impressive R&D spending figures. But here is what those figures conceal: pharmaceutical companies routinely **spend more on marketing and administration than on research.** And much of what they call "R&D" includes the cost of developing minor variations of existing drugs — so-called "me-too" drugs — that offer no meaningful clinical benefit. A study cited by the **Open Markets Institute** found that **nearly half of FDA-approved drugs between 2005 and 2011 lacked any tangible health benefit,** such as prolonging life or relieving symptoms. We are paying premium prices for marginal — or nonexistent — improvements.

---

### 2. Monopoly Power, Not Innovation, Drives Prices

My second argument explains **why** prices are so high: not because innovation is expensive, but because pharmaceutical companies have constructed an elaborate system of **monopoly protections** that eliminate competition.

The **Open Markets Institute** documents this with devastating clarity. Between 1995 and 2015, **60 pharmaceutical companies merged into just 10,** creating an industry dominated by a handful of giants with enormous market power. These companies then deploy an arsenal of tactics to maintain their monopolies long beyond what patent law intended:

- **"Pay-for-delay" agreements**, where brand-name manufacturers pay generic competitors to stay off the market
- **Patent thickets** — filing dozens or hundreds of minor patents on a single drug to extend exclusivity
- **Manipulating the FDA's patent system** to block generic entry

The **U.S. House Committee on Oversight and Reform** produced a 269-page report revealing how pharmaceutical companies systematically manipulate the prescription drug market. It found that companies use revenue not primarily for R&D but to **suppress competition and extend monopolies.** In the case of insulin — a drug discovered over 100 years ago — three American manufacturers have increased prices by **168%, 169%, and 325% respectively** since 2010. This is not innovation justifying a price. This is a **cartel** exploiting patients who will die without the product.

The historical contrast is instructive. During the post-World War II "wonder drug" era, when competition was actively encouraged, the price of **penicillin dropped from $3,995 per pound in 1945 to just $282 by 1950.** When markets are competitive, drug prices fall. When monopolies reign, they soar. The difference is not R&D cost — it is **market structure.**

---

### 3. The "Innovation Will Suffer" Argument Is a Myth

My third argument confronts the pharmaceutical industry's most powerful weapon: the threat that lowering prices will kill innovation. The evidence shows this threat is **empty.**

In 2022, the United States passed the **Inflation Reduction Act**, which for the first time allowed Medicare to negotiate drug prices directly with pharmaceutical companies. The industry warned of catastrophic consequences for research. What actually happened? As *Vox* reports, **R&D spending by the pharmaceutical industry actually increased** after the law's passage. Companies whose drugs were selected for negotiation noted in their own shareholder reports that the negotiations **would not materially impact their revenues.** The sky did not fall.

Moreover, the claim that private industry alone drives pharmaceutical innovation is fundamentally misleading. Research cited by **STAT News** reveals that **every single drug approved between 2010 and 2016 relied on science funded by the National Institutes of Health** — that is, by taxpayers. Public funding lays the scientific foundation; private companies then develop, patent, and price the resulting drugs. As Torreele puts it, we have a system where the **public pays twice** — once through taxes that fund the basic research, and again through high prices at the pharmacy counter.

The **Congressional Budget Office** estimated that aggressive price negotiation might reduce new drug approvals by **9% over 30 years** — roughly 1 to 2 fewer drugs per year. That is a meaningful but modest cost. And it must be weighed against the **immediate, massive, and measurable** harm of current prices: patients rationing insulin, families declaring bankruptcy over cancer treatment, and health systems worldwide straining under unsustainable drug costs.

---

### 4. Patients Are Dying Because of These Prices

My fourth argument moves from economics to human reality. High drug prices are not an abstract policy debate — they are a matter of **life and death.**

**Dr. Otis Brawley** of Johns Hopkins describes the crisis facing American patients: people are **rationing insulin** — splitting doses, skipping injections, diluting their medication — because they cannot afford it. Some of them die. Families facing a cancer diagnosis are simultaneously facing **financial ruin**, with novel anticancer drugs routinely costing more than **$100,000 per year** in the United States.

As reported by the **Kaiser Health Tracking Poll**, **72% of American patients** believe drug costs are unreasonable, and **74%** believe pharmaceutical companies prioritize profits over people. These are not radicals — they are patients.

And it is not just an American problem. Gilead's hepatitis C treatment, when it launched in 2014, cost **$84,000 for a three-month course** — roughly **$1,000 per pill.** Torreele notes that the production cost of this drug was **"just a few hundred dollars"** — meaning the price was roughly **50 times** the cost of manufacturing. Current treatments for cancer and rare diseases now reach **€500,000 to over €2 million.** A sickle cell disease gene therapy costs **€3 million or more.**

At these prices, even wealthy nations cannot treat all their citizens. In the developing world, millions of people simply go without. The HIV/AIDS crisis of the 1990s — when antiretroviral drugs existed but cost **€10,000–15,000 per year** while millions in Africa died — should have taught us this lesson permanently. High drug prices do not just inconvenience patients. They **kill** them.

---

### 5. The System Serves Shareholders, Not Patients

My fifth argument reveals what high drug prices actually fund — and it is not primarily innovation.

Torreele describes a pharmaceutical industry that has been captured by **financialization** — where the primary purpose of drug development is not to cure disease but to generate returns for shareholders. Companies engage in massive **stock buyback programs,** returning profits to investors rather than reinvesting in research. They spend more on **marketing** — persuading doctors and patients to use their products — than on developing new ones.

The **New York Magazine** investigation into pharmaceutical lobbying found that in 2021 alone, the industry spent **$124 million on 846 lobbyists** — roughly **two lobbyists for every member of Congress.** These lobbyists work relentlessly to block legislation that would lower drug prices, protect patent monopolies, and ensure that the system continues to deliver extraordinary profits. The investigation revealed PACs funded by lobbyists tied to **AbbVie, Johnson & Johnson, Eli Lilly, and UnitedHealth** — designed not to advance public health but to protect the industry's pricing power.

The **U.S. House Oversight Committee** found direct links between drug price hikes and **executive compensation increases.** In at least some cases, companies raised prices on existing drugs — drugs whose development costs had been recovered years ago — specifically to boost revenue numbers that triggered executive bonuses. The three insulin manufacturers even **coordinated their price increases,** with a particularly notable round of hikes occurring around Christmas 2015.

This is not a system that prices drugs based on the cost of innovation. This is a system that prices drugs based on **the maximum the market will bear** — and the market will bear almost anything when the alternative is death.

---

### Conclusion

Let me close with a historical fact that should haunt every defender of pharmaceutical pricing.

When **Jonas Salk** developed the polio vaccine in 1955 — one of the most important medical breakthroughs in human history — he was asked who owned the patent. His answer: **"The people. Could you patent the sun?"** Salk understood something that the modern pharmaceutical industry has forgotten: medicines exist to **serve humanity,** not to maximize quarterly earnings.

The high prices set by pharmaceutical companies are not the inevitable cost of innovation. They are the product of **monopoly power**, artificially extended through patent manipulation and pay-for-delay schemes. They are inflated by an industry that spends more on **lobbying and marketing** than on discovering new treatments. They are set not by what innovation costs, but by **what desperate patients can be forced to pay.**

And they are killing people. Patients ration their insulin. Families go bankrupt over cancer treatment. Entire nations in the developing world are priced out of life-saving medicines. This is not a system that rewards innovation — it is a system that rewards **exploitation.**

Insulin was discovered in 1921. It should not cost a family their savings in 2025. The high prices set by pharmaceutical companies are not justified — they are a **moral failure** disguised as an economic necessity.

Vote against this motion. Thank you.
