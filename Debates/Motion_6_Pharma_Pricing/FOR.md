# FOR the Motion: "The high prices set by pharmaceutical companies on drugs are justified"

## Speech (~8 minutes)

---

Ladies and gentlemen,

In 2019, a drug called **Trikafta** was approved by the FDA. It treats cystic fibrosis — a devastating genetic disease that once meant a death sentence before age 30. Trikafta costs **$311,503 per year.** That is an eye-watering number. But here is what that number bought: for the first time in history, patients with cystic fibrosis can expect to live into their **50s, 60s, and beyond.** Children who were once planning funerals are now planning careers. Parents who counted every breath can now count on decades with their children.

That drug did not appear out of thin air. It took **decades** of research, billions of dollars in investment, and thousands of failed experiments before a single patient swallowed a single pill. And someone — investors, researchers, companies — had to be willing to take that enormous financial risk, knowing that the overwhelming majority of drug development programs fail.

I stand firmly in support of this motion: **the high prices set by pharmaceutical companies on drugs are justified.** Not because pharmaceutical companies are charitable organizations — they are not — but because the system that produces these prices is the same system that produces the medicines that save our lives.

---

### 1. The Staggering Cost and Risk of Drug Development

My first argument is about what it actually takes to bring a drug to market. The public sees a pill and a price tag. They do not see the **decade or more** of work that preceded it.

According to **PhRMA**, the industry's research trade association, its member companies spent **$96 billion on research and development in 2023 alone** — representing **21% of their combined global revenues.** The total biopharmaceutical R&D ecosystem invested between **$276 and $288 billion globally** in 2024. To put that in perspective: the entire GDP of Finland is roughly $300 billion. The pharmaceutical industry spends nearly the equivalent of a developed nation's economy — every single year — on research.

But here is the critical fact that critics routinely ignore: **only about 1 in 10 drugs that enter clinical trials ever reaches patients.** As **Louise von Stechow and Markus Thunecke** of the consulting firm Catenion note in *Financier Worldwide*, development costs range from **$1 billion to several billion dollars per drug,** and the success rate is brutally low. When a company prices a successful drug, it is not just recovering the cost of *that* drug — it is recovering the cost of the **nine drugs that failed** alongside it. PhRMA data shows that across the industry, **over $5 billion in total spending** is needed for every single drug that eventually reaches approval.

The panelist from the model debate who argues against the motion will likely point to high prices and call them exploitative. But they must answer this question: who will fund the next breakthrough if the successful drugs cannot pay for the failures?

---

### 2. Innovation Is Real — and Accelerating

My second argument addresses the claim that high prices do not actually produce innovation. The data tells a very different story.

**Matthew Herper**, a senior writer at *STAT News*, provides a powerful comparison: between 1980 and 1999, the FDA approved **55 cancer drugs.** Between 2000 and 2019, that number nearly tripled to **147 cancer drugs.** This is not stagnation — this is an **explosion** of innovation that has transformed cancer from a death sentence into a manageable condition for millions of patients.

In 2023 alone, the FDA approved between **40 and 50 new drugs.** These are not trivial improvements — they include breakthrough treatments for conditions that were previously untreatable: gene therapies for sickle cell disease, new immunotherapies for previously fatal cancers, treatments for rare genetic conditions that affect children.

The industry's own investment patterns confirm the link between pricing and innovation. PhRMA reports that **development-stage companies** — smaller, riskier firms working on the drugs of tomorrow — invest **$73 billion annually,** representing over 25% of total R&D spending. These companies have no revenue from existing products; their entire business model depends on the expectation that successful drugs will be priced high enough to justify the risk. Remove that expectation, and the pipeline of future medicines dries up.

---

### 3. The Global Innovation Subsidy — Why America Pays More

My third argument explains a fact that critics constantly raise but rarely understand: **why U.S. drug prices are higher than in other countries.**

Research from **Stanford Graduate School of Business** confirms that U.S. drugs cost **2 to nearly 4 times** as much as in Canada, Mexico, and many European countries. Critics present this as evidence of exploitation. In reality, it is evidence of a global free-rider problem.

In countries like Germany, France, and the UK, governments negotiate directly with pharmaceutical companies and set maximum prices — often using reference pricing systems that anchor to what other countries pay. The UK's NICE, for example, applies a threshold of roughly **£30,000 per quality-adjusted life year (QALY).** These price controls mean that European patients benefit from American innovation — **at a discount.**

The result is that the **United States effectively subsidizes pharmaceutical innovation for the entire world.** The U.S. accounts for **55% of global biopharmaceutical R&D investment,** despite representing only about 4% of the world's population. Europe contributes 29%, and Asia/Pacific just 15%. American patients pay more because American investment drives the research that produces the medicines everyone uses.

Is this system fair? Perhaps not. But the solution is not to lower American prices to European levels — because if you do, the math no longer works. As **STAT News** reports, the Congressional Budget Office estimated that aggressive price negotiation could reduce new drug approvals by **up to 15 drugs over a decade.** The President's Council of Economic Advisers put the estimate even higher: **100 fewer drugs.** Those are not abstractions — those are treatments for someone's cancer, someone's child's rare disease, someone's parent's Alzheimer's.

---

### 4. The Pricing System Is More Complex Than Critics Admit

My fourth argument challenges the misleading simplicity of the "list price" narrative. The actual price patients and health systems pay is far more nuanced than headlines suggest.

As **Natalie Healey** writes in *Raconteur*, "It's easy to attack pharmaceutical giants for their eye-watering profits, but list prices for new medicines **don't give the full picture.**" The U.S. drug pricing system involves complex negotiations between manufacturers, insurers, pharmacy benefit managers (PBMs), and government programs. The list price — the number that makes headlines — is rarely the price actually paid.

**Dr. Andrew York**, Executive Director of the Maryland Prescription Drug Affordability Board, emphasizes that "it's critical to understand how the payment system works" before drawing conclusions. The American Association of Colleges of Pharmacy notes that **over 66% of pharmacists** have taken on new responsibilities specifically to help patients navigate pricing and find savings — because the system, while byzantine, does contain mechanisms for affordability.

Moreover, the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 introduced **Medicare drug price negotiations** for the first time, with agreements reached on 10 selected drugs by August 2024. Crucially, as *Vox* reports, **R&D spending by the pharmaceutical industry actually increased** after the law's passage — contradicting the industry's own doomsday predictions and demonstrating that moderate price reform and continued innovation can coexist.

---

### 5. The Alternative Is Worse — Consider the Counterfactual

My fifth argument asks the most important question in this debate: **compared to what?**

Critics point to the pre-patent era as evidence that innovation does not require high prices. **Els Torreele** of University College London notes that insulin, penicillin, and the polio vaccine were developed "without patent and profit prospects." This is true — but deeply misleading.

The diseases we have already cured — the "low-hanging fruit" — were often amenable to relatively simple interventions. The diseases that remain — Alzheimer's, most cancers, ALS, treatment-resistant depression, rare genetic disorders — are **orders of magnitude more complex.** Modern drug development requires genomic sequencing, artificial intelligence, international clinical trials with thousands of patients, and manufacturing processes of extraordinary sophistication. The cost of developing a drug in 2025 is simply not comparable to the cost in 1955.

Consider also what happens when pharmaceutical investment declines. The **Open Markets Institute** notes that between 1996 and recent years, average annual new drug approvals dropped from **54 to about 30.** This decline coincided with a period of increasing consolidation and cost-cutting in the industry. When companies face pricing pressure, they do not heroically maintain R&D — they cut it. They merge. They focus on the safest, most profitable drugs rather than the riskiest, most transformative ones.

The panelist from the model debate who argues against the motion will offer a vision of publicly funded, affordable drug development. It is an appealing vision. But the reality is that **no government on Earth** currently has the capacity, infrastructure, or risk tolerance to replace the private pharmaceutical industry's $276–$288 billion annual R&D investment. Until one does, high prices are not just justified — they are **necessary.**

---

### Conclusion

Let me close with a simple truth that this debate too often forgets: **every drug that saves a life was once an investment that might have failed.**

The $311,503 annual price of Trikafta reflects not just the cost of developing Trikafta. It reflects the cost of every cystic fibrosis treatment that was tried and failed over 30 years. It reflects the cost of the clinical trials, the regulatory approvals, the manufacturing facilities. And it reflects the cost of ensuring that there will be a **next** Trikafta — for the next disease, for the next generation of patients.

Are pharmaceutical companies perfect? Absolutely not. There are legitimate concerns about patent manipulation, about excessive price increases on older drugs, about marketing spending. These deserve scrutiny and reform. But the core question of this debate is whether **high prices on drugs are justified** — and the answer, when you understand the extraordinary cost, risk, and complexity of pharmaceutical innovation, is yes.

The price of a drug is high. The price of **not having that drug** is incalculable.

I urge you to support this motion. Thank you.
